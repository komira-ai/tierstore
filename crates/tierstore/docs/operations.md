# Operations

## Starting

Start the unit by hand (see [install](install.md)). The process does not test or probe its tiers (see [What tierstore requires of a store](operations.md#what-tierstore-requires-of-a-store)): it opens the proxy and binds the address. Opening the proxy reads and writes the hot tier's own state, so a hot tier that cannot be reached fails the start (exit 1, `opening the proxy: ...`); the cold tier is first asked by the first request that needs it. When it is ready it prints one line on stdout:

```text
tierstore <version> listen=192.0.2.10:9000
```

With the OCI front on, the line gets ` oci-listen=<address> (https)` or ` (http)` after the listen address. With more than one address of a kind the value is every address, in the order given, separated by commas with no space: `listen=0.0.0.0:9000,[::]:9000`. Every other message goes to stderr, and so to the journal.

## Probes

Four paths of the S3 listener answer a GET or HEAD without a signature, so a bucket cannot have any of these names.

| Path | Answer | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| `/healthz` | 200 `ok` while the process runs | liveness |
| `/readyz` | 200 `ready` until the drain begins, then 503 `draining` | a load balancer's health check |
| `/usagez` | 200 with the age of the hot tier's published usage, or 503 with the reason | alerting |
| `/metricz` | 200 with the counters, in the Prometheus text format (see [Metrics](operations.md#metrics)) | scraping |

`/readyz` does not look at the usage: one stopped reconciler would otherwise make every node unready. `/usagez` is for alerting, not for a load balancer.

The 200 of a build node says which mode its cache is in: `ready read-write` or `ready read-only` (see [the cache lock](operations.md#the-cache-lock-and-read-only-mode)). The status is the same in both: a read-only node serves.

## Usage

The reconciler publishes the hot tier's usage every pass. `/usagez` says:

```text
usage published <N>s ago
no usage yet, <N>s since start
usage of the hot tier is stale: published <N>s ago, limit <M>s
usage of the hot tier is missing: none published in <N>s, limit <M>s
```

The first two are 200 and the last two 503. The limit is `hot_store.usage_stale_after` (`hot_store.usage_stale_after`, 2m by default). A server that starts before the reconciler's first pass is 200 for that long. Puts ignore a usage older than 30 seconds, as if the hot tier were not full, so a stopped reconciler means a hot tier that is never taken to be full. Puts are not refused; alert on `/usagez`.

## Eviction and the water marks

The hot tier's size is measured against `hot_store.budget`. Three shares of it matter:

- Above `hot_store.high_water` (80% by default) the reconciler evicts the oldest objects that the cold tier already holds, until the hot tier is down to `hot_store.low_water` (70%). It never evicts an object it has not copied.
- Above `hot_store.critical` (95%) a read of an object that is only in the cold tier is served but not copied back into the hot tier.
- At the budget, or when the hot tier says it is full, new objects are written straight to the cold tier, and so are new multipart uploads ([Multipart uploads](operations.md#multipart-uploads)).

Eviction frees the hot tier only as fast as the cold tier takes copies, which `hot_store.copy_parallel` bounds. If the cold tier is slow or down, copies stall, nothing becomes evictable, and the hot tier climbs to the budget.

## The reconciler's lease

One reconciler works at a time: the one that holds `_tiering/lease` in the hot tier. It lists `_entries/`, copies what the puts leave to the cold tier, finishes the deletes handed over to it, copies a cold object into the hot tier for each copy-back a read asked for, evicts, reaps the entries of owners that departed, and publishes the usage. Beyond the lease and the usage it publishes it keeps nothing in the tiers: what it knows it reads again from the entries, so a reconciler that is stopped or loses the lease loses nothing but its place in a listing.

While it works, the holder renews the lease on a timer of its own, every `hot_store.lease_renew` (a third of `hot_store.lease_ttl` unless that is set). A step that has less than `hot_store.lease_margin` (a third of `hot_store.lease_ttl` unless that is set) left on its lease (the margin) does not start a copy to the cold tier or a delete until the lease has the margin again: if it still holds the lease (the timer's renewal is under way or late) it renews it at once, and starts the work if that succeeds; if the renewal fails, or the lease was lost, the item fails and waits for the next step. The lease is not a fence: a step that loses the lease (a renewal failed because the hot tier did not answer it, or another reconciler took the lease) may still finish a copy beside the new holder. The copy is create-only and the entry is changed on its content tag, so the new holder finds what was done and does not do it twice.

Another reconciler takes the lease over when it has seen the lease object unchanged for `hot_store.lease_ttl` by its own monotonic clock, counted from the first time it looked. The clocks of two machines are never compared, so they may differ by any amount. A process that has just started therefore waits at least `hot_store.lease_ttl` after its first look before it takes a lease that a crashed holder left, and takes it on a step after that, so the step interval and the calls to the hot tier add to the wait; a holder that stops normally gives the lease up, and the next step of another takes it at once.

An eviction deletes on the content tag the copy found (`If-Match` where the store enforces it), so an object that was put again with other bytes after the copy is kept. A reconciler that took over from another has no tag for the objects the other copied: it first checks that the cold tier holds a real object of the key with the same size and the same bytes as the hot object. The bytes are compared (both objects read in ranges of at most 1 MiB, each read with its content tag, which must be the one the delete uses, so a key replaced and put back around the read is not deleted), and not the tags across tiers, since tiers of different kinds tag the same bytes differently. If it does not, the hot object stays, since it may be the only copy, and it remains a candidate that the next step asks about again. A tag names content: an object put again with the same bytes has the same tag.


## Multipart uploads

A multipart upload is made in the hot tier whenever it has room, like a put, and is copied to the cold tier by the reconciler after its completion. Only when the hot tier is full (at the budget, or answering full to the start) is a new upload made in the cold tier. The tier is chosen when the upload is created and cannot change: its parts and its completion go to the same tier, which the upload id says. The id is opaque to a client. An id has a `ts-hot~` prefix for a hot upload and none for a cold one, so an upload begun by the release before this one is a cold upload and goes on as before.

- **The hot tier fills up in the middle of an upload.** The parts already stored are in the hot tier, so the upload stays there. A part is accepted until the hot store itself has no room; then it is answered 503 `SlowDown` and nothing of it is stored. Send the part again later, or abort and start again (the new upload goes to the cold tier while the hot tier is full). A completion that finds no room to make the object (a `dir` hot tier joins the parts into a new file, so it needs room for the whole object) is answered 503 `SlowDown` the same way, and the upload stays open for the same completion to be tried again.
- **The parts are not in the usage.** The usage counts objects, and parts are not objects until the completion, so uploads in flight take hot space the budget does not see. Keep `hot_store.budget` below the real capacity by the uploads you expect at once.
- **A key that exists.** A completion of a key that either tier holds is answered 412, as a put is, and the upload stays open (abort it). Two clients completing one key: one wins, the other gets 503 while the winner is in flight and 412 after.
- **An upload nobody completes** is kept until its client aborts it, as in S3, where an incomplete upload and its parts stay (and are billed) until the client aborts it or the bucket owner sets a lifecycle rule. Its parts hold hot-tier space that the usage does not count. To have such uploads aborted, set `hot_store.abandoned_upload_after` (for example `"168h"`): the reconciler's stray sweep (at takeover and daily) then aborts the uploads of the hot tier that have stood open longer, which is what a lifecycle rule `AbortIncompleteMultipartUpload` does for an S3 bucket (set one on the cold bucket as before; on an S3 hot bucket either works, and on a `dir` hot tier only the key does). The default `"0s"` aborts none. Parts of an upload in a `dir` hot tier are in `.uploads/` under its root.
- **ListMultipartUploads** lists the uploads of both tiers, the cold tier's uploads of a key before the hot tier's. A janitor with a GC key lists and aborts them by the ids it is given.
- **During an update** a client that began an upload on a node of this release and sends a part to a node that still runs the release before is answered `NoSuchUpload` (that release does not know the prefix), and starts the upload again. An upload that began on the release before goes on on this one.

## What a full disk does

A `dir` hot tier whose disk is out of space, or an `s3` hot tier that answers 507, is a full hot tier: the put that met it goes to the cold tier where nothing of its body was taken yet, and the tier is taken to be full for a moment. A put that had already streamed some of its body into the hot tier fails, and the client's retry goes to the cold tier. Reads use both tiers throughout. The hot tier is used again once the usage falls. An object written straight to the cold tier has its entry like any other, and the reconciler finds it in the cold tier and has nothing to copy.

If the cold tier is a full disk, puts that reach it fail. A tier that is full at start still starts. Size `hot_store.budget` below the real capacity of the SSD: the budget is what the process compares with, not what the file system has.

## Drain and restart

On SIGTERM or SIGINT (what `systemctl stop` sends) the process drains:

1. `/readyz` answers 503 at once, and every answer from then on carries `Connection: close`, so that a client does not send its next request on a connection that is about to end;
2. for `server.drain_lb_wait` (10s), the settle, the server goes on accepting connections and serving everything, so that a load balancer sees the 503 and a connection that was routed here a moment ago is served, not reset;
3. at the end of the settle it closes each connection that has no request on the wire, and stops accepting connections without resetting one the kernel has already set up for it: it takes and serves the connections whose handshake is over or still in flight, and closes its socket once none is left, `server.door_grace` (3 seconds) at most (always that long once the host's network namespace has answered any connection with a SYN cookie, and on any system but Linux, where it cannot see every handshake). Send new connections elsewhere before the stop (the update hook moves its redirect off an instance before it stops it): a connection that comes as the socket closes is reset. A new connection that has not sent its first request yet is given the same `server.door_grace` to begin it, and that request is served, with `Connection: close`; a `server.drain_deadline` shorter than that wins. Each connection it closes gets a lingering close: it shuts its end for writing, so that the client reads a clean end of stream, and goes on reading until the client closes its end, or for `server.header_timeout` (60 seconds) at most (the time an idle connection is let to wait for its next request), and then closes;
4. a request that is on the wire at that moment (begun, and not answered in full) is served, with `Connection: close`, never answered 503, and its connection is closed in the same way once the answer is out;
5. it waits for the requests in flight and for the connections to close, at most `server.drain_deadline` (15m) in all;
6. it gives up the reconciler's lease and exits 0.

A request that arrives within one round trip of the end of the stream, on a connection that was idle since before the drain began, cannot be answered: the client sent it before it read the end of the stream. The server reads and drops it and counts the connection in `tierstore_drain_late_requests_total`. A client that follows `Connection: close` never meets this; a client that retries such a request must be sure the request is safe to repeat, which a create-only put is not.

The OCI read front (an `oci` interface) drains the same way and at the same moments: from the stop its answers carry `Connection: close`, it goes on accepting and serving for `server.drain_lb_wait`, a blob being sent when the settle ends is sent in full, and then each of its connections gets the same lingering close, for 60 seconds at most. A connection that lingers still holds its place under `params.max_connections`, and one still open at `server.drain_deadline` is cut and counted in `tierstore: <N> OCI connections were cut at the drain deadline`. A client that writes on an OCI connection after its end of stream is counted in `tierstore_oci_drain_late_requests_total`, beside the S3 listener's count.

A request cut at the deadline is counted on stderr: `tierstore: <N> requests were cut at the drain deadline`. So are the connections that sent a request too late, as the process exits: `tierstore: <N> connections sent a request too late in the drain of the S3 front`, and the same with `the OCI front`, a line only for a front that counted one. These are the last values of `tierstore_drain_late_requests_total` and `tierstore_oci_drain_late_requests_total`, which count connections: they can rise from the start of the drain, but reach their last value after the settle, when `/metricz` has closed with the S3 listener. Another process over the same tiers takes the reconciler's lease once it is given up or has expired (`hot_store.lease_ttl`). State is in the tiers, not the process, so a restart needs nothing but its file.

On a Mac, a build node (a file with `[local_cache]` and `[cold_store]`) sent SIGUSR2 (`sudo launchctl kill SIGUSR2 system/tierstore`) hands over to the next image of its own program instead of exiting: it drains as above, except that its listening sockets stay open (a connection that comes meanwhile waits in the socket's backlog, and is not reset), its cache starts no write and lets the writes in flight end, and the process then executes its program path (where an update installed the new binary) with the same arguments and `--handed 0`. The pid stays, so launchd sees no exit and starts nothing. The new image takes the listening sockets and the cache's lock from its standard input, serves on the same addresses, and says `ready read-write` once it has taken the write role (the stage emptied, the objects scanned again); until then a miss is served from the upstream without a fill. It says its start line again on standard output. If the new image refuses what it was handed (its file names other addresses), it exits 1 and launchd starts the job again, which binds the addresses itself; a new binary that cannot start at all needs the old one put back. `--handed` is the process's own flag: given by hand to another shape of file it is refused with status 2. The update hook does not send SIGUSR2 yet. A build of macOS lists `handoff-exec-v1` in `--capabilities`.

To restart for a new binary or a new key file, replace the file, then `systemctl restart tierstore`; run more than one process, with ids of their own, if clients must not see the pause. The update hook (`tierstore update-hook`) does that for a host updater; see [the update hook](update-hook.md).

During an update two releases share the hot tier (and, on a node with `[local_cache]`, the cache directory), and each reads what the other writes. The objects and files tierstore keeps of its own (the key entries, the registrations, the format object, the markers, the cache sidecars, the update hook's record and the rest), where each lives and the rule that each changes only by adding are in [docs/stored-formats.md](https://github.com/komira-ai/tierstore/blob/main/docs/stored-formats.md).

`hot_store.lock_file` says which of the processes over one node reconciles. A process that holds an exclusive lock on the file runs the reconciler and takes the lease. A process that finds the lock held serves reads and writes through its own proxy at once and does not touch the reconciler's state in the hot tier, the lease included, until it holds the lock; it tries every `hot_store.lock_retry` (one second unless that is set). The system frees the lock when the holder exits or crashes, so a process started beside the one that is draining reconciles as soon as that one has exited and given its lease up. Give both processes the same file, on a local file system, in a directory that nothing removes: a directory under `/run` that systemd removes with the unit would let a third process make a new file and lock it beside the second. The holder looks before each pass at whether the path still leads to the file it locked; if the file was replaced, it gives its lease up and waits for the lock on the new file. A start whose lock file cannot be opened, is a link or is on a network file system exits 1. Without the flag the reconciler works as soon as the process starts, as before.

## Live update by hand-off of the sockets

With `server.handoff_socket` (see [configuration](configuration.md)) a storage node, a build node or a storage node with `[local_cache]` is replaced by a new process of the same settings without a gap and without root: the new process takes the listening sockets of the one that runs, so no connection the kernel holds is reset. Without the key a start binds its own sockets and makes no control socket, as the releases before did. A start with the key opens its proxy, and then:

1. it connects to the control socket. Where there is no file, or a file no process listens on (one left by an instance that was killed), it starts cold: it binds its own sockets, as without the key;
2. where an instance answers, it asks for that instance's sockets and checks that their addresses are the ones its own settings name (an address with port 0 takes the port the instance has). It takes them, including the loopback sockets that a node with `[local_cache]` adds to its S3 addresses; the running instance removes the control socket and begins its drain, as on SIGTERM (see [Drain and restart](operations.md#drain-and-restart)): it finishes the requests in flight and exits 0, and until its settle ends both processes accept on the same sockets;
3. it makes the control socket itself, mode 0600 (a file left at the path is replaced), so that the next start takes from it. A process that stops with no successor removes the file.

A start fails, binds nothing and exits 1, naming the hand-off, when the running instance refuses (another start is taking its sockets, or the start runs as another user), offers other addresses than the settings name (a live update cannot change the addresses: stop the running instance first), or does not end the hand-off within `server.handoff_wait` (10s). A running instance that accepts the connection and never answers is not a cold start: a bind beside it would fight it for the ports.

The running instance's `done` is the point after which it drains, so a start makes every check that can fail before it: before it asks, that the control socket's directory exists and this user may write it, that the path is not a directory, and that its sockets are no more than one message carries (253); before its `ack`, that it can take each socket and copy it for its own offer (a start that cannot answers `nak`, and the running instance goes on). Each is a failure of the start, exit 1, with nothing bound. Once `done` is said nothing fails the start: if the control socket cannot be made then, the start says so on stderr and serves without one, and it cannot be handed over until it restarts. A running instance whose taker answered `ack` but was gone before `done` reached it has handed over nothing: it makes its control socket again (mode 0600) and goes on serving; if it cannot, it says so on stderr and goes on serving without one.

The two processes run side by side for the old one's drain, each with its proxy; `hot_store.lock_file` decides which of them reconciles, as above. On a node with `[local_cache]` the new instance serves read-only (it does not hold the lock of the cache directory, and `/readyz` says `ready read-only`) until the old one exits, and then takes the lock and is read-write (see [the cache lock](operations.md#the-cache-lock-and-read-only-mode)); on a combined node `hot_store.lock_file` and the lease decide the reconciler as before. On a node with `[local_cache]`, `server.handoff_socket` must not be inside `local_cache.path` (exit 2). Keep the control socket in a directory only the node's user may write: the unit's `StateDirectory`, `/var/lib/tierstore` (mode 0700, `packaging/hostkeeper/units/tierstore@.service`). A taker of another user is refused. The package sets neither key: the node's configuration names the socket, and the update hook's `prepare` refuses an update without it (see [the hand-over socket](update-hook.md#the-hand-over-socket)). On a Mac set neither: an update there is a restart (see [the update hook](update-hook.md#on-a-mac)). A build that has the hand-off lists `handoff-socket-v1` in `tierstore --capabilities`, and one that hands off the cache shapes lists `handoff-cache-v1` too.

## The format object and the pause of writes

The hot tier's object `_tiering/format` says what a proxy may do. A proxy reads it at start, after each heartbeat and, at most every 5 seconds (the proxy's `format_refresh`), before a write; a read that fails leaves what it last read. A read is never paused.

- `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`, or a later epoch: writes are made on entries (below).
- `{"phase":"drain"}` or `{"phase":"drained"}`: every proxy answers a put, a delete and the multipart calls that write with 503 `ServiceUnavailable` and the text `writes are paused while the store changes format; retry later`, and goes on serving reads. `drained` does not show that every proxy has seen the pause, and it does not wait for a write that a proxy admitted before it read `drain` and that is still running.
- `{"phase":"active-marks","epoch":3,"marks_bucket":"<name>"}`: writes are made on the markers of their keys in the marks bucket `<name>`, and not on entries ([Rolling out the markers](operations.md#rolling-out-the-markers-and-going-back)). A proxy whose `hot_store.marks_bucket` is another name, or that has none, answers writes 503 and logs why. Reads are never paused.
- `{"phase":"drain","epoch":2}`: the pause over the entry protocol (`--drain` over `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`). It pauses writes like `drain`, and the leader finishes every open entry and then writes `{"phase":"drained","epoch":2}` (below). That `drained` carries the epoch, an optional field of the format that every reader accepts and ignores, so that `--cancel` of it can put `active` at epoch 2 back; the `drained` the bridge release writes has none. A `drain` with no epoch is the pause before the entries, under which the leader of this release does nothing.
- `{"phase":"drain","epoch":3,"marks_bucket":"<name>"}`: the pause of a rollback from `active-marks`. It pauses writes like `drain`, and the leader goes on finishing the markers of that bucket under it; `drain` with no `marks_bucket` is the pause before an activation, under which the leader does nothing.
- An `active` with no epoch or an epoch below 2, or a phase this release does not know: writes are answered 503 with the text `writes wait until the format object says active at epoch 2 (tierstore format-epoch --activate); reads are served`.
- No object: the hot tier is new, and the first start writes the format itself (below). Until it has, for example while the hot tier cannot be read, writes are answered 503 with the same text.

**A hot tier with no format object is new.** At start, after its first beat, a node whose hot tier has no format object writes it with a create-only put and goes on; there is no operator command and nothing is listed first. An `s3:` hot tier gets `{"phase":"active-marks","epoch":3,"marks_bucket":"<name>"}` naming its marks bucket (`hot_store.marks_bucket`, `<bucket>-marks` unless named); the bucket must exist, and if it does not, writes fail with the errors of a missing bucket (nothing probes it). A `dir:` hot tier gets `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`, as `--activate` writes it. A `memory` hot tier is given the active format before the proxy opens. When nodes start at once one put wins and the others read the object and follow it; an answer to the put that may or may not have been applied is not retried, and the node reads the object at once and at every beat. A format object that exists, of any phase, is never touched by a start. **Unsupported:** a release from before the format object, run against a bucket that a release with this rule has used (it wrote journals that nobody applies). That release only ever ran on the farm, which is past it.

The verb `tierstore format-epoch` (below) writes and removes this object, with checks.

The release that reads this object (the bridge release) must run on every node before anything writes `_tiering/format`: a node of an earlier release does not read it, and goes on writing.

Once the object says `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`, a proxy makes every put on the journal entry of the key (`_entries/<sha256 of the key>` in the hot tier): a put, a streamed or presigned put, and the completion of a multipart upload. The entry decides which of two puts of one key wins; the loser is answered 503 while the winner is in flight, and 412 once it has landed. A put of a new key is answered 503 while the cold tier cannot be asked, because the cold tier is where "this key does not exist yet" is decided. A proxy puts on entries only while its heartbeat is acknowledged. How many times a put looks again at an entry that vanished between its create and its read is `hot_store.entry_rounds` (3). The last step of a put (the replace that acknowledges it) and of a delete (its hand-over, or the removal of its entry) is tried again when the hot tier fails it without a definite answer: up to `hot_store.step_attempts` (4) tries, after sleeps of `hot_store.step_retry_min` (100 ms) doubling to `hot_store.step_retry_max` (2 s), and the entry is read before a replace is sent again, so a replace that had landed is not repeated and a put is acknowledged only for a replace known to have landed. A delete is made on the entry too: the cold tier's object is deleted at once and then the hot tier's, each call once. A delete that finds no entry removes its own entry afterwards. A delete that finds a landed put or a requested copy-back hands the entry to the reconciler (`(DEL, PENDING)`) and is acknowledged. A delete that finds a put the reconciler is copying, or a delete handed over (a retry of one that failed), hands the entry over and then waits until the reconciler has released it, so that a copy that was in flight cannot outlive the delete; if the entry is still there after the wait it answers 503 (both tiers are deleted, and the retry waits again). A delete that finds an entry that is held or sealed and is not one it can amend or claim (a copy-back, eviction, put or delete the reconciler has sealed, or a put or a delete of another client in flight) changes nothing yet and waits in the same way until the entry is released, and then deletes both tiers; if the entry is still there after the wait it answers 503 and nothing was deleted. The wait is `hot_store.delete_wait`: 30 seconds in all, reading the entry after sleeps of `hot_store.delete_poll_min` (1 second) doubling to `hot_store.delete_poll_max` (10 seconds). The reconciler of the same release works the entries from the same moment (copies what a put leaves, finishes a handed-over delete, evicts, and does a copy-back), as [Rolling out the entry protocol](operations.md#rolling-out-the-entry-protocol) says.

## The write pause of a format change: format-epoch and entries

Two verbs of the binary run the one planned pause of writes and look at the journal entries of keys. They are on main, not in a release yet. Each is a short-lived process, answered before the server's flags are read, that takes the hot tier by the server's flags (`--hot`, `--hot-endpoint`, `--hot-region`, `--hot-credentials-file`, `--s3-request-timeout`). They read and write only tierstore's own objects in the hot tier (`_tiering/format`, `_tiering/proxies/`, `_entries/`); they never ask a store how it behaves, so what the stores must do is documented, not checked. The credential they run with needs create, read, replace and delete on those prefixes.

`tierstore format-epoch` takes exactly one of six actions. Run from a machine that is not a proxy, with the same clock discipline as the proxies, since a heartbeat's age is the difference of that machine's clock and the hot tier's time of the write.

- `--check` prints the format object (its phase and epoch), each registration with how it is classified (and the phase it reports and its writes in flight, when its body says them), and the number of open entries, then says whether `--drain` would be accepted. It writes nothing. It exits 1 with the refusal if `--drain` would be refused.
- `--drain` writes `{"phase":"drain"}` and waits, with no time limit, until the format says `drained`, reading it every `--poll` (5 seconds) and printing what it waits for. If the format is at an epoch, the epoch is kept; it refuses an `active` record at an epoch above 2, which no verb writes, and `--check` says so. Interrupt it to stop waiting; the pause stays. Run it again to wait again: a format that already says `drain` is not written over.
- `--drain` over `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`, the entry protocol of this release: the reconciler that holds the lease writes `{"phase":"drained","epoch":2}` itself. It finishes every open entry as it does under `active`, and writes `drained` once `_entries/` is empty and every registration reports that it starts no write: a `phase` of `drain` or `drained` and `writes_in_flight` of 0 (the proxy reports both at every beat, within `hot_store.format_refresh` and one beat of the pause). The registrations are read before `_entries/` is listed, so no entry can appear after the listing that finds none. The write is over the content tag of the format object, so a `--cancel` that lands first wins and nothing is written. A registration that does not report both (a release that does not write them, a process that has not beaten since it started) holds the drain until its process has departed: its registration is gone, or has stood unchanged for `hot_store.departure` (60 seconds by default) by the leader's clock; `--departed` only lets the verb write the pause, and the leader's rule decides when the registration is no longer waited for; delete the registration object of a process you have checked is gone to end the wait at once. Each poll prints the open entries and each registration that holds the drain, with why.
- `--drain` over a store that still has journals to apply (a format with no epoch): the reconciler of the bridge release, the one before this release, writes `drained` after it applies the journals; this release's reconciler does nothing under a `drain` with no epoch, so `--drain` there needs the bridge release running.
- `--cancel` resumes writes: it deletes the format object, or, if the format says an epoch (a `drain` or `drained` over the entry protocol), writes `active` with that epoch back, also after the leader has written `drained`. It refuses when the format is not `drain` or `drained`.
- `--activate` writes `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}` over `drained`, after the checks below. On a store that has no format object it creates the object instead, and refuses if the store has a `_journal/` object of a release before the entries: such a store is drained by the bridge release first. (A new hot tier does not need it: the first start writes the format, above.) It does not rotate the write credential; it prints that the operator does, and that processes of the new release get the new credential first.

- `--activate-marks` writes `{"phase":"active-marks","epoch":3,"marks_bucket":"<name>"}` over `drained`, after the checks of [Rolling out the markers](operations.md#rolling-out-the-markers-and-going-back): the format is `drained`; every live registration is protocol 3 or later, reports that it acts on `drained` and reports no write in flight (a write admitted before its proxy saw `drained` would make its entry after the format says `active-marks`, with no marker); no entry under `_entries/` and no `_journal/` object exists; the marks bucket (`--marks-bucket`, `<hot bucket>-marks` unless named) can be listed and holds no object; and the operator has passed `--old-release-fenced`. It refuses in the dry run too when the flag is missing, and says what the rotation is for. A hot tier that is not `s3:<bucket>` has no marks bucket in this release, and the verb refuses it.
- `--restore-entries` writes `{"phase":"active","epoch":2}` over `drained`, once no proxy can write a marker: every live registration of protocol 3 or later reports a phase of `drain` or `drained` and no write in flight, no entry exists, and the marks bucket holds no object.
- `--drain` over `active-marks` is the way back, and needs `--marks-bucket` to name the recorded bucket. It writes `drain` keeping the epoch and the bucket, and then waits, with no time limit, for every live registration of protocol 3 or later to report a phase of `drain` or `drained` with no write in flight; only then does it look at the marks bucket, deleting the `WANT` markers it finds (a request for a copy-back owes nothing) and waiting until the leader has run every other marker to its end; it writes `drained` once the bucket is listed empty. A registration that goes silent while it waits stops it with the ids to name on `--departed`; run it again. `--cancel` of this pause writes `active-marks` back.

`--drain`, `--cancel`, `--activate`, `--activate-marks` and `--restore-entries` are held: without `--apply` they make every check, print what they would write, and write nothing. A write is over the content tag that was read, so a format object changed by someone else in between is refused, not overwritten.

A registration (`_tiering/proxies/<id>`) says whether its process is alive only if its body has a protocol number and the object is rewritten on every beat. A registration is live when it has a protocol number and was written less than `--beats` (3) times `--beat` (5 seconds) ago, silent when it has one and was not, and unlabelled when its body has none, as the registration of a release before the heartbeat. A silent or unlabelled registration says nothing of whether its process is gone, and a process of an old release that goes on writing during the pause is what the pause is meant to rule out. So `--drain` and `--activate` refuse until the operator has looked at the machines and named each such id on `--departed` (repeat the flag or separate ids by commas). `--departed` refuses an id that has no registration and an id that is live. `--activate` also refuses a live registration below protocol 2.

Before the pause: tell the owners of the clients that write (builds, image pushes), since writes answer 503 for as long as the drain takes, which grows with the number of keys to copy and does not end while the cold tier is down. Run `--check` first, with nothing at stake. After `--drain` has printed `drained`, roll the new release over the proxies, then `--activate`. To go back before `--activate`, `--cancel`.

`tierstore entries ls` prints a line for each open entry (`_entries/<sha256 of the key in hex>`): the name, the operation (`PUT`, `DEL`, `CB`, `EVICT`), the state (`HELD`, `PENDING`, `SEALED`), the owner (`-` for none), the whole seconds since the entry was last written, and the key, quoted and escaped. An entry whose body cannot be read is listed with the reason. `tierstore entries show --key <key>` prints the entry of one key, or says there is none. Both only read.

## Rolling out the entry protocol

The entry protocol is the older of the two write formats this release knows. The newest is the marker format (the format phase `active-marks`), which a store is moved onto after it is on the entry format: see [Rolling out the markers and going back](operations.md#rolling-out-the-markers-and-going-back). This section is the rollout of the entry protocol from the journals.

This release has the entry protocol only: the per-node journals, their checkpoints and the journal reconciler are gone. It reads no `_journal/` object and writes none, and it takes no write until the format object says `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`. So it is rolled over a store after the bridge release has drained the journals, and the store is activated once. There is no canary, because the epoch is one object that every node reads: the whole fleet changes protocol at once, after one planned pause of writes.

What a node of this release does about the epoch:

- At start, and then after every heartbeat (`hot_store.heartbeat`, 10 seconds), a proxy reads `_tiering/format` and keeps what it says. A write or a read that finds the kept answer older than `hot_store.format_refresh` (5 seconds) reads it again. So a node takes up the epoch within a few seconds of its being written, a node that starts after it is written takes it up at its first beat, and a node that cannot read the object keeps what it last read.
- Its registration says protocol 2 (the release before says 1). `--activate` refuses while a live registration says less, so a node still on the old release is found before anything is written.
- Its reconciler does nothing, and takes no lease, until the epoch is active. From then on it works the entries: a round, then the next at once while a round changes something, and otherwise after a wait of `hot_store.idle_min` (one second) that doubles up to `hot_store.idle_max` (ten seconds) while nothing is to do. The reconcile interval of the releases before is gone (a file that still sets it loads, and the start says it is ignored); the wait is these two keys. A hot object that an old journal described and no entry names is adopted by the stray sweep once it is older than `hot_store.stray_grace` (ten minutes).
- The retry delays, sweep and usage intervals, listing and copy-back limits of the entry reconciler, and the proxy's usage limit, format refresh, looks at an entry and delete wait, are `[hot_store]` keys with the defaults of the releases before (see [configuration](configuration.md)); `hot_store.departure`, `hot_store.lease_ttl`, `hot_store.copy_parallel` and `hot_store.budget` apply to them too. So are the idle wait of the loop, the lease's renewal and margin, the size of a listing page and the waits of a stop, a lock retry and the first heartbeat. The 20-doubling cap of a retry stays fixed. A release that does not know these keys reads a file that sets them, warns of each, and uses its own defaults.

The order, once, in a window agreed with the owners of the clients that write:

1. Before anything: the pre-flight of the design. A lifecycle rule `AbortIncompleteMultipartUpload` on the cold bucket (and on an S3 hot bucket, though the reconciler's sweep covers the hot tier too); the hot credential allowed the multipart calls (`s3:AbortMultipartUpload`, `s3:ListMultipartUploadParts`, `s3:ListBucketMultipartUploads`) besides `s3:PutObject`; the hot credential allowed `_entries/*` and `_tiering/proxies/*`; on a versioned hot bucket, expire noncurrent versions after one day, since every put leaves a created and a deleted entry. Every node runs the bridge release (it reserves `_entries/`, heartbeats and answers `drain` with 503). Run `tierstore format-epoch --check` and read it.
2. Tell the owners of the clients that write. Writes answer 503 from the next step until step 5, which takes as long as the journals take to apply and does not end while the cold tier is down; reads are served throughout.
3. `tierstore format-epoch --drain --apply`. It waits until the format says `drained`, which the reconciler of the bridge release writes once every journal is applied. (This is the store that still has journals; a store already on the entry protocol drains as in [Rolling out the markers](operations.md#rolling-out-the-markers-and-going-back).) Name on `--departed` each registration the check lists as silent or unlabelled once you have looked at its machine.
4. Roll this release over every node (the update hook hands the sockets over, so no connection is refused). A node of this release serves reads and answers writes 503 while the format says `drained`.
5. `tierstore format-epoch --check`, then `tierstore format-epoch --activate --apply`. Within seconds every proxy puts and deletes on entries, and writes are accepted again. This ends the pause.
6. Rotate the write credential as `--activate` prints, so that a process of an older release that is restarted later cannot write: new-release processes get the new credential first. An old binary started after the epoch writes journals nobody reads; that is not supported.
7. Watch `tierstore entries ls`: entries appear while a put or a delete is in flight and are gone within seconds. An entry that stays names its owner; one whose owner has departed is reaped by the reconciler after `hot_store.departure`, and one whose owner has restarted under the same `server.proxy_id` once the owner's registration has stopped naming the process that made the entry (a restarted proxy names the earlier process for `hot_store.departure` of its heartbeats, because that process may be draining beside it as in a hand-off, and not after), at the next reconciler step; and one whose owner is still beating in the process that made it but has left it held (a put or delete whose hand-over failed) after `hot_store.held_ttl` (one hour).

A new store has no journal to drain: install this release and start it; the first start writes the format and the node takes writes.

To go back before step 5, `tierstore format-epoch --cancel --apply` and roll the bridge release back over the nodes: a node of this release answers writes 503 while the format is not active. Going back after step 5 is not in this release.

**The old objects.** The `_journal/<proxy id>/` objects and the `_tiering/checkpoint/` objects of the releases before stay in the hot bucket, untouched, so that a rollback to the bridge release still finds them; they only take space. The release after this one is to delete them. Do not delete them by hand before then.

## Rolling out the markers and going back

The release after the entry protocol replaces the entry of a key with a marker: one object named by the key in a second bucket of the hot store, the marks bucket, which says that a change to the key is not yet the cold tier's too, or that a process holds the key. A put no longer takes a lock; only an overwrite, a delete, an eviction and a copy-back take one. The design is `docs/design/put-without-lock.md` (sections 4 and 8). This release carries the verbs that move a store onto the markers and back; **it still carries the entry protocol**, which a later release removes once the farm has run on markers. Until then a store that stays at `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}` behaves as before.

**The activation is one planned pause of writes**, once: a proxy of the release before the markers answers 503 to every write beside `active-marks` (and ignores markers on a read), so the format is drained first, the release that knows markers is rolled, and then the format is activated. Reads are served throughout. Plan it as the pause of [Rolling out the entry protocol](operations.md#rolling-out-the-entry-protocol): tell the owners of the clients that write.

Before the drain, once:

1. Create the marks bucket on the hot store: `<hot bucket>-marks`, or the name you set in `hot_store.marks_bucket`. Grant the credentials of the proxies list, read, write and delete on it, as on the hot bucket. It must exist **before the new release is rolled**, not only before the activation: from `drained` on, a read that misses the hot tier reads the marker, and a bucket that does not exist makes a read of a key only the cold tier has answer 503. A `dir` hot tier has no marks bucket in this release.
2. The cold tier is up. The markers are copied to it, and the way back needs it.

The order:

1. `tierstore format-epoch --drain --apply` with the release that has the entry protocol running, over `{"epoch":2,"phase":"active"}`. Writes answer 503; the leader finishes every open entry, and writes `drained` once `_entries/` is empty and every registration reports a pause and no write in flight (the verb prints what it still waits for at each poll). No one writes `drained` by hand.
2. Roll the release that knows markers over every node (the hand-over of the sockets, as for any update). A node of it reads the marker on every hot miss from `drained` on, answers writes 503, and registers protocol 3 with the `phase` it acts on and its `writes_in_flight`.
3. Fence the old release: rotate the credential it uses on the hot and the cold store, giving the new credential to the processes of this release first. An old process that starts after the activation does not read markers, and so serves the cold copy of a key that was deleted. The verb cannot see the credential, so it asks you to say it is done.
4. `tierstore format-epoch --activate-marks --marks-bucket <name> --old-release-fenced`, a dry run: it prints each check that holds and the record it would write. Read it. The refusals are the point of the dry run; each names what to change: the format is not `drained`; a registration is below protocol 3, or has not yet reported `drained`, or still reports a write in flight or no count of them (wait for its next beat, 10 seconds by default); a registration is silent and not named on `--departed`; an entry or a journal object exists; the marks bucket cannot be listed or holds an object; the old release is not fenced.
5. The same with `--apply`. Within `hot_store.format_refresh` (5 seconds) every proxy writes on markers; a proxy configured with another `hot_store.marks_bucket` answers writes 503 and logs the recorded name until its configuration matches.
6. Watch `/metricz` and the hot bucket's request rates as for any change of protocol. A put of a new key makes one request more to the marks bucket than before to the hot bucket (see the design, section 6).

**Going back** (the markers cannot be converted; they are run to completion):

1. `tierstore format-epoch --drain --marks-bucket <name> --apply`. It writes `drain` over `active-marks` and waits as above. It prints what it waits for each poll: a proxy that still acts on `active-marks` or has writes in flight, or the count of markers by state. A key deleted under the markers is carried to the cold tier (its `gone` marker is cleared after `hot_store.tombstone_hold`, two minutes by default), so the wait is at least that long when a delete was recent. It needs the leader to be running with the same marks bucket; with none it waits for ever (interrupt it; `--cancel --apply` puts `active-marks` back).
2. When it prints `drained`, the marks bucket is empty and no proxy writes a marker. Roll the release before the markers back if it is to run.
3. `tierstore format-epoch --restore-entries --apply`. Needs the cold tier up. Because this release still has the entry protocol, its proxies go on writing on entries; a release without the entry protocol could not, and the release before the markers must then be rolled back before this step.

The marks bucket stays, empty; delete it when you will not go forward again. The release before the markers never reads it.

A write that is in flight when the rollback begins and that a proxy has already admitted is counted in the registration's `writes_in_flight` and waited for (a delete is counted until its lock on the marker is let go, even if its client has gone away); a write that begins after the proxy has read `drain` is refused. An object such a write already landed, whose marker was not written, is adopted by the stray sweep of the entry protocol after `hot_store.stray_grace`.

## What tierstore requires of a store

tierstore is a proxy over a hot store and a cold store. It does not test either store, at start or later: it does not write probe keys, and it does not check that a store behaves as described here. A store that does not meet these requirements is not detected; the guarantees below are the ones that are lost, silently. (Opening the proxy at start reads and writes the hot tier's own state, the node's registration, so a hot tier that cannot be reached fails the start: that is the proxy's work, not a test of the store.)

The hot store must provide:

- an atomic conditional PUT: create-only (`If-None-Match: *`, which creates the object or fails with 412 when the key exists) and `If-Match` (which replaces the object only if its `ETag` is the one given, and fails with 412 otherwise). The lease, the checkpoint and the registration of the proxies are written this way, so a store that ignores either header lets two processes both believe they hold the lease or that a put is the first;
- strong read-after-write consistency for GET, HEAD and LIST: a read, a head or a listing that follows a successful write sees it, and one that follows a delete does not see the object.

The cold store must provide the same create-only PUT (`If-None-Match: *`), on a single PUT and on the completion of a multipart upload, and an atomic `If-Match` PUT, also on the completion of a multipart upload (a body over `server.s3_single_put_max`, 5 GiB by default, or of unknown length, is sent as one), which the reconciler uses to replace a reservation with its object. A store that overwrites on a create-only write loses the one-winner rule of a put, and one that ignores `If-Match` can let the reconciler overwrite a replacement of the key.

Known to meet these requirements: Amazon S3, MinIO (for the conditional PUT; use a release that has it) and tierstore's own `dir` store, which meets them among the calls of one process only: its conditional writes are atomic inside one process, so a `dir` hot tier that two processes use at once (a live update, a second process over the same node) is not covered by the store: `hot_store.lock_file` (see above) is what keeps two such processes from reconciling and holding the lease together, and an S3 hot tier does not need it. The `memory` store of a process meets them too, but its objects are lost when the process stops.

tierstore does not verify any of this. Run a store you have not seen meet them through your own tests before it holds data. A cold store that cannot be reached is not an error of the start: the first request that needs it fails as an ordinary request does (503 `ServiceUnavailable` for the client, and a retry by the reconciler), and the process goes on.

### Conditional delete

The proxy and the reconciler delete an object only if it still has the content tag they read (`Backend::delete_if_match`). How that is sent to an `s3:<bucket>` tier is the tier's `conditional_delete` setting (`hot_store.conditional_delete`, `cold_store.conditional_delete`), which tierstore trusts and does not ask the store about:

- `true`: a DELETE with `If-Match: "<tag>"`. A store that enforces the header refuses a wrong tag with 412 and removes the object only on the right one. Set it for a store that enforces `If-Match` on a DELETE, such as Amazon S3. A store that takes the header and deletes anyway (some S3-compatible stores do) must not have it set: the fence is then no fence.
- `false`: a HEAD, a comparison of the content tags and a plain DELETE. It is right on every store and leaves a window in which another writer's replacement of the key between the two requests is deleted.

The default for an S3 tier is `false`: tierstore does not detect Amazon S3 from its endpoint (an S3-compatible store is reached at any address), so the default is the one that is right on every store, and `conditional_delete = true` is for the operator to set. A `memory` tier and a `dir` tier compare the tag and remove the object in one atomic step: their setting is `true`, and `false` is refused when the file is loaded. A guarantee that rests on conditional deletes holds only once every process over the tiers runs a version that makes them; during a rolling upgrade a process of an older version deletes without a tag. The key is new in this release, and the release before refuses a file that has it (it refuses every key it does not know, status 2): leave `conditional_delete` out of a file until no process that may be started from it runs the release before. A file without the key loads in both releases (`crates/compat/tests/config.rs`).

### What earlier releases did at start

Releases before this one ran a self-test of each tier at start, under probe keys in `_tiering/selftest/`, and exited 3 for a tier that answered it wrongly and 1 for one that could not be reached. This release does neither. `cold_store.skip_checks` and `server.backend_check_wait` are still accepted, ignored, and each given is one line on stderr saying so (see [configuration](configuration.md#deprecated-settings)). Probe keys that an earlier release left under `_tiering/selftest/` (a start that was killed) are not deleted by this one; they are small and harmless, and may be deleted by hand.

## A build node

A build node (the file has `[local_cache]`, see [configuration](configuration.md#a-file-for-a-build-node)) serves the S3 front as a read cache. What it keeps: a key is kept if the longest `[[policy]]` prefix that holds it says `cache = true` (and it is not under `_journal/`, `_tiering/` or `_entries/`). A GET or HEAD of a kept object is answered from the disk with no call to the upstream. A GET of one that is not there is read from the upstream in chunks of 8 MiB into a staging directory, hashed as it passes, and linked into place only when it is whole; the misses of one key share one fill. A ranged GET of a miss fills the whole object if it is at most 64 MiB, and otherwise passes the range through and keeps nothing. A fill that fails never fails the reader for the cache's reasons: the reader is answered from the upstream. Every call but GET and HEAD is refused 403 `AccessDenied`.

Put `cache = true` only on prefixes whose objects are never rewritten, the content-addressed ones: a kept object is never checked against the upstream again. Anyone who may read through the front may read what the upstream's key can read, so give clients scoped keys.

Memory is bounded for the fills: at most `local_cache.max_concurrent_fills` chunks of 8 MiB are held. A miss that finds every slot taken is served from the upstream and kept nowhere; if the count of such skips climbs, raise the key. A response is held whole in memory, as for any backend.

Eviction keeps the cache under `local_cache.budget`: above `high_water` percent of it, or when the free space of the file system is under `free_floor` percent, a pass (every 10 seconds) removes the least recently used objects down to `low_water` percent and until the free space is back at the floor. The free-space floor only triggers a pass; it does not stop a fill. An object that another tree has linked is skipped, counted and stays charged to the budget. A fill is skipped when the object is larger than the budget or would take the cache above `critical`.

At start the process makes the directory, removes what a fill left half done, reads the kept objects back (sizes only; an object whose sidecar does not say what the file holds is deleted), and asks the file system four questions with a probe object. A file system that answers one wrongly exits the process with status 3, naming the check. The upstream is not asked anything at start. On SIGTERM it drains as a storage node does.

### The cache and a power cut

The cache is a copy, so a fill, and the copy of a put, do not ask the disk to make the bytes durable: no `fsync` (on macOS, no full flush of the drive, which costs about 10 ms a blob; a cold attach of 12,394 manifests took 134 s with it). What survives a power cut is whatever the disk took; the jobs that were running are not restarted, and the process starts again with what is on the disk.

A file a power cut left short must not be served, and the size is what says so: the size of every object is recorded when it is kept (in its sidecar, and in the index, which has it from the scan), and is compared with the size of the file.

- **At start** the scan deletes an object whose file is not the size its sidecar says, or whose sidecar does not parse (counted in `tierstore_cache_discarded_at_start_total`).
- **At every open** of a file the cache serves, and at every fill request the filesystem process makes, the size of the file is compared with the recorded one, which costs one `fstat` of a file that is opened anyway. An object whose file is another size (shorter, or longer) is dropped and fetched again from the upstream: the read is a miss, and the fill request is not answered `ok`.

The bytes are never hashed on a read. A file of the right size whose bytes a power cut left as zeros is therefore not found by this check; it is found only by the scrub (not in a release yet, see [limits](limits.md)), and until then it is served until it is evicted. The pins file is written the same way and is not synced either: a pins file the power cut left unreadable is ignored and said on stderr, and the trees are pinned again when their clients attach. Only the cache is relaxed. What a tier of the node stores as its truth (a `dir` tier's objects and sidecars, which are synced before a put is answered) and the state of the update hook are still written durably.

### The cache lock and read-only mode

Two processes may have one cache directory open at once, for example a new binary started beside the one that is draining. Only one of them writes to it. The directory has a lock file, `.lock`, and the process that holds an exclusive lock on it is read-write: it fills, keeps, evicts and records the use of objects. A process that finds the lock held is read-only. A lone process takes the lock before it binds the address, so it is read-write from its first answer; the system frees the lock when the holder exits or crashes.

A read-only process serves what its scan of the directory found, from the disk, and sends every other read and every write to the upstream. It makes and removes nothing in the directory and stamps no file: it does not empty the staging directory or delete a half-published object at start, skips the start-up probe of the file system, deletes nothing when a read of a kept object fails (the read is answered from the upstream), and starts no fill, no keep of a put, no eviction pass and no write of file times. A create-only put that reaches it with `write_through` goes to the upstream and succeeds, with no copy made in the directory; it is counted as `read_only` in `tierstore_cache_write_keep_skipped_total`. An object the holder keeps after the scan is read from the upstream until the process takes the lock. An object the holder deleted or put again after the scan is too: before it serves a hit (or answers a HEAD) from the disk, a read-only process checks that the file is still the one its scan found, the same length and a sidecar with the same size and tag, and reads any other from the upstream, removing nothing; an object named by its content (`cas/l/<sha>`) is not checked, since its bytes cannot change. The counters of fills and evictions (`fills_total`, `evictions_total`, `fill_failures_total`) stay at 0 in a read-only process; hits, misses and bytes count.

With `write_through`, a delete or put sent to a read-only process goes to the upstream, and from then on the process reads that key from the upstream. The files the holder has for it stay on the disk, because the process may not remove them; the process notes the names, at most 65536 of them. When it takes the lock it removes those files after its scan and before it is read-write, so a deleted or replaced object is never served from the files the holder left. If more than 65536 keys were deleted or replaced, the process does not know which, and it removes every object file when it takes the lock and starts with an empty cache. One window is left: the holder itself still serves its own cached copy of such an object on the connections it is draining, until it exits. It ends with the holder's drain (`server.drain_deadline` at most).

A cache that loses its lock (the lock file was replaced, see below) stops writing at once: a fill or the copy of a put (a keep) that is running publishes nothing, and the put is counted as `read_only`, and neither touches the staging files of the process that holds the directory now, which are named with a prefix of their own. The loss is noticed by the one-second try, so for up to that second a fill or a keep still believes it owns the directory. A fill or keep that has linked its object into the directory when it finds the loss removes the data file and the sidecar it linked (they are its own: no other process makes those names), so the object is not left where the new holder does not index it. What is left of the window is a whole, valid object that is on the disk for the moments until that removal.

The process tries the lock every `local_cache.lock_interval` (one second unless that is set). When it takes it, it empties the staging directory, scans the directory again (deleting what is not a whole object) and only then is read-write; stderr says so, and the first line of the start says which mode it started in. `/readyz` says the mode in its body and `tierstore_cache_mode` in the metrics (see [metrics](metrics.md)); the status of `/readyz` does not depend on it. A holder looks at each try whether `.lock` still leads to the file it locked: a file that was removed or replaced is a lock that no other process looks at, so the holder lets it go, is read-only, and takes the new file when it can. A lock file that is a link, or on a network file system, stops the start (exit 1); a try that fails later is said on stderr and made again.

### The fill socket

A build node also listens on a Unix socket for the filesystem process of the same node (`tierstore fs`, see [the virtual build file system](operations.md#the-virtual-build-file-system)), which asks it to put blobs in its cache. The socket is `local_cache.fill_socket` (default `fill.sock` in the service's state directory), mode 0600, and a peer that is not the user of the process is answered `refused` and disconnected. It is bound only by the process that holds the cache lock: a read-only process binds nothing, and a process that takes the lock binds in the tick in which it takes it, replacing the file the process before it left. During a live update the old process answers through its drain, and the new one binds when the old one has exited; a client that finds the socket closed asks again. A process that loses the lock closes the socket. The requests are one line each, answered one line each in order on a connection (a client that wants fills to run together opens several connections):

- `fill <sha256> <size> from <key> @ <offset>`: read `<size>` bytes from `<offset>` of the upstream object `<key>` in chunks of `local_cache.fill_chunk`, hash them as they pass, and keep them as the cache entry named by `<sha256>` (the entry the object `cas/l/<sha256>` has) only if they hash to `<sha256>`. A key under `_journal/`, `_tiering/` or `_entries/` is refused as a source, and `<size>` is at least 1. A blob cut out of a larger object is recorded with origin `span` and is not served to S3 or OCI clients as the object `cas/l/<sha256>` (a read or `HEAD` of that key goes to the upstream); the filesystem process opens its file in the cache directory. A request for a digest that has a fill running (from this socket or from a read of the S3 front) waits for that fill and takes no permit. A cache directory of the previous release has its fill entries (key `_tiering/fill/<sha256>`) renamed to the digest at start, or removed when the digest name is taken (a pin saved under the old name goes to the digest entry). A request that finds a client's put of the same key running waits for the put to end and then fills. A client's put of `cas/l/<sha256>` over a `span` entry of the digest turns it into an object and keeps its pins; a delete of that key leaves a `span` entry alone, since no upstream object was deleted. To roll back to the release that kept fills under `_tiering/fill/<sha256>`, empty the cache directory first: that release serves an entry named by a digest as the object `cas/l/<sha256>`, `span` entries included. The answer is `ok`, or why not: `upstream <status>` (the upstream failed or has no such object; 404, 503 and so on), `mismatch` (the bytes are not the digest), `short` (the upstream gave fewer bytes than asked), `no space` (larger than `local_cache.budget`, or past `critical`), `busy` (no permit came free within `fill_socket_wait`; counted in `tierstore_cache_fill_socket_busy_total`, and said on stderr at warn, at most once in 10 seconds with the number of answers it stands for), `read-only` (this process does not hold the cache lock, or lost it during the fill), `io` (the directory could not be written), `changed` or `aborted`.

The fill socket's waits are visible: `tierstore_cache_fill_socket_waiting`, `tierstore_cache_fill_socket_running` and `tierstore_cache_upstream_reads_in_flight` in [metrics](metrics.md). A watchdog looks once a second; when requests have waited for a permit with no read of the upstream in flight for 30 seconds, it says so on stderr at warn (`N fill requests have waited S s with no upstream read in flight (M fills hold a permit)`), again every 30 seconds while that lasts, and at info `the fills move again` when it ends.
- `fill <sha256>`: the same for the whole object `cas/l/<sha256>`, up to 64 MiB (`too large` above it), which is the file a tree's manifest is. It is the object a read of that key through the S3 front fills. A delete or put of that key through the S3 front that comes while the fill reads it makes the fill answer `changed` and keep nothing, as it does for a fill that a read started.
- `touch <sha256> [<sha256> ...]`: count those blobs, or objects `cas/l/<sha256>`, as used now, for the order of eviction. Never answered, and a word that is not a digest is passed over.
- `pin <mount> <tree> <sha256> [<sha256> ...]`, `unpin <mount> <tree>` and `pins-live <mount> [<tree> ...]`: the pins of the cache (see `local_cache.pin_limit`). A mount and a tree are each one word of at most 128 visible ASCII characters: the id of the filesystem process's mount, and the digest of the attached tree. `pin` keeps those blobs against eviction for the tree and is answered `ok <pinned> <refused> <absent>` (the blobs now pinned by the tree, those refused because the pinned bytes would pass `pin_limit` (each is counted as used instead), and those the cache does not hold); a blob is named by the SHA-256 of its bytes, and it is the one entry of that digest, whether a `fill` or a read of `cas/l/<sha256>` kept it. `unpin` takes away every pin of the tree and is answered `ok <blobs>`. `pins-live` takes away every pin of the mount that is not of a tree listed (and every pin of another mount) and is answered `ok <trees>`. A process that does not hold the cache lock answers `read-only`.
- `put <key> <sha256> <size> from <path>`: put the file at `<path>` (an absolute path; the rest of the line, so it may hold spaces) as the object `<key>` of the upstream, create-only, through the cache as a client's create-only put goes: under a prefix the cache keeps, the node keeps a copy of what it put. `<key>` is one word, not under `_journal/`, `_tiering/` or `_entries/`; `<size>` may be 0. The file is opened without following a link and must be a regular file of `<size>` bytes; it is read once and hashed as it is sent, and a body that does not hash to `<sha256>` is cut before its last byte, so nothing is stored. To an S3 upstream the digest is also sent as `x-amz-checksum-sha256`, which the store checks (a single PUT against the whole body; a body over `server.s3_single_put_max` is a multipart upload whose every part carries its own checksum), and recorded as the object's metadata `x-amz-meta-sha256`. A key that exists is not overwritten: the request is a success if the object there is `<size>` bytes with that SHA-256 (the same put sent again after its answer was lost), judged by the checksum the store keeps for the whole object, or by the recorded digest for an object made of parts (whose kept checksum is of the parts). No HEAD is sent before the put. The answer is one line of JSON, not plain words: `{"ok":true,"created":true}` (made), `{"ok":true,"created":false}` (the same bytes were there), or `{"ok":false,"error":"<word>"}` with `exists_different` (other bytes are there), `checksum` (the file is not `<size>` bytes or not `<sha256>`, or the store refused its checksum), `toolarge` (the store refused the object for its size), `stage` (the file cannot be opened or read, or is not a regular file) or `upstream` (anything else: the upstream did not answer, a cache that does not write through, the object deleted while the answer was decided; a retry may succeed). A put takes no permit of the fills.

- The requests of the filesystem process about the node's [placements](configuration.md#trees) at fs locations (see [the virtual build file system](operations.md#the-virtual-build-file-system)), answered from the node's tree store. `place <mount> <path>`: where the path of the mount is; `<path>` is the rest of the line, names joined by `/`, the first not `trees`, `views` or `put`. The answer is `tree <placement>` for the fs location of a placement that is alive and not released, which the node then holds for the mount (an attachment of the run `<mount>`) until `unplace <mount> <placement>` (answered `ok`); `dir` for a path a location is below; `none` otherwise. `mounted`: the paths of every such location, one line of JSON (`["jobs/1f3a/root"]`). `flat <placement>`: the placement's tree whole, one line of JSON: the paths of its fs locations, the keys of its packs, and every entry (the root first, depth first, children by name; for a tree of a version 2 list, in the list's order) with its parent, name, stored mode and kind, a file with its size, SHA-256, pack and offset; or `none` (no such placement, or none with an fs location), `damaged` (a file of the tree cannot be read) or `too large` (more than 2^24 entries). `image <placement>`: for a tree of a version 2 list, `image <tree> ["jobs/1f3a/root"]`, the tree's digest and the paths of the placement's fs locations: the filesystem process reads the list from `trees/<tree>/image` in the cache directory, which the placement it holds keeps there, and numbers each entry by its place in the list; for any other tree, the answer to `flat`. A node of the release before answers `image` `refused unknown request`, and the filesystem process asks `flat` instead. `places-live <mount> [<placement> ...]`: ends the holds of the mount on every placement not listed, answered `ok <holds ended>`; the filesystem process sends it after `pins-live` once a start has made its attachments, so the holds of a process before it that ended without giving them back are not kept. A start does not wait for the two: it tells the service manager it is ready, and a thread of the process sends them once the fill socket answers (after a boot the node's `tierstore` reads its whole cache before it binds the socket), asking again until it does; every `pin`, `unpin`, `place` and `unplace` of the mount waits for their answers first, so neither undoes one made after the start. `span <sha256> <size> from <pack> @ <offset>` (the words of a `fill` of a blob): the file of the cache that holds those bytes of the pack, filled first and checked against `<sha256>` if the cache does not hold them (a fill of a range of more than one `local_cache.fill_chunk`, such as a large file's span, asks the upstream for up to four chunks at once, in the order of their offsets, and hashes and writes them in that order), answered `ok <offset> <file>` with the offset of the first byte in the file, which is the pack's own entry: the bytes are not copied out of it, and no entry is made for `<sha256>`. A span that cannot be had is answered with why (`fill: upstream 404`, `fill: mismatch`, `fill: no space`, ...). A node with no tree store answers `place`, `flat`, `image` and `span` with `none`, `mounted` with `[]` and `places-live` with `ok 0`; a node of the release before answers them `refused unknown request`, which the filesystem process takes as no placement.

A line that is not a request is answered `refused` and the reason (`unknown request`, `malformed request`, `not a sha256 digest`, `not a number`, `size is zero`, `offset and size pass 2^64`, `not a usable key`, `reserved key`, `not a usable name`, `not an absolute path`, `not a path of the mount`, `not a placement id`, `the request is not text`); a line longer than 64 KiB is refused and its connection closed. The fills of the socket have a pool of permits of their own, `local_cache.fill_socket_concurrency` (16), and wait for one (a `put` takes none: its connection runs one request at a time, and the client chooses how many connections it opens); they never take one of `max_concurrent_fills`, which a miss of the S3 front takes without waiting, and the S3 front never takes theirs. Concurrent requests for one blob share one fetch. A request whose client has gone away still finishes its fill. A fill is counted in the cache's ordinary series (`fills_total`, `fill_failures_total` by reason, `upstream_reads_total`). An `ok` is about the moment it was given: the cache may evict the blob after, and the next request for it fetches it again.

### The prefetch queue

A build node's cache keeps a queue of blobs to fill before a read asks for them, and workers that fill them (not in a release yet; the hydrate call below is what queues blobs). A blob in the queue is in the same table of running fills as a fill that has started, under the entry's name (the digest), so that a request for it finds it:

- A request of the fill socket, or a `GET` of the S3 front for an object `cas/l/<sha256>`, that finds the blob **queued** starts it at once, on a permit of its own pool (`fill_socket_concurrency`, or `max_concurrent_fills` without waiting), ahead of the blobs before it; the queue forgets it, and the blob is fetched once. An S3 read that finds every permit of its pool taken is answered from the upstream and leaves the blob queued. A queued blob cut out of a pack is not an S3 object, as a running one is not: an S3 read of its digest goes to the upstream and does not start it.
- A request that finds the blob **running** waits for that fill and takes no permit. This is counted in `fill_joins_total` by path.
- The workers are the permits of `local_cache.prefetch_concurrency` (default 2), a pool that no read draws on and that no read waits for; with every permit of the two pools of the reads taken a worker still fills, and with every worker busy a read still fills. When a worker's fill ends, the blob that has waited longest starts on its permit.
- A blob is checked as its turn comes, not when it is queued. It is dropped without a request if the cache holds it by then, if it has waited longer than `local_cache.prefetch_queue_ttl` (default 120 seconds: the job has most likely read it; `prefetch_queue_ttl` is counted on the cache's clock, and a blob that has waited exactly that long is filled), or if the bytes held, the bytes of the fills that run and its size would pass `high_water` (a prefetch never evicts what may be in use for what may be read; the fill itself still stops short of `critical`). A tree larger than the cache is therefore filled in the order it was queued until it is full.
- At most `local_cache.prefetch_queue` blobs (default 16384) wait; a blob that finds the queue full is turned away `busy`. A blob larger than `local_cache.budget` is turned away `too_large`; a read-only cache fills nothing; `prefetch_concurrency = 0` turns the queue off.
- **A tree is expanded into the queue** (not in a release yet; the hydrate call below is what expands a tree). The cache reads the tree as an attach does, the root manifest `cas/l/<tree>` and the manifest of each directory below it, through the fill socket's fill (so an attach of the same tree that comes while a manifest is fetched joins the fetch, and the reverse, and a manifest read here is in the cache for the attach that follows), and hands every file with bytes to the queue as the walk meets it, in manifest order, from its `source` (the object `cas/l/<sha256>` where it has none). A directory listed at several places is read and walked once; an empty file and a link need nothing; the walk keeps the stack of the manifests it is listing and the digests of the directories it opened, not the tree. A tree sent again costs no request for a manifest or blob the cache holds.
- **A tree the cache refuses** is not expanded and does not stop the other trees of the call: a manifest the upstream does not have, that the cache cannot read, that is larger than 64 MiB, that does not hash to its digest, or that the library refuses (an unknown `v` or entry kind, a bad name, a tree of distinct manifests over 16777215 entries or deeper than 4096 levels), counted `refused` in `hydrate_trees_total`. A file whose `source.key` is under `_journal/`, `_tiering/` or `_entries/` is skipped and counted `reserved_key`; the walk goes on. A call made with a scoped key expands only what the key may read: a tree whose manifest is outside its read prefixes is not expanded, and is counted `access_denied` (the manifest is not fetched), and a file whose `source.key` is outside is left out and counted `access_denied`.
- **The bounds of a call** are `local_cache.hydrate_max_trees` (default 64: a call that lists more is refused whole, before anything is read; a hold is for at most that many trees over all its calls), `hydrate_max_blobs` (default 500000) and `hydrate_max_bytes` (default 32 GiB). The last two count the blobs and bytes a hold newly queues, all its trees and all its calls together; a blob the cache holds or that is queued or running already is free. The walk stops at the first blob it would newly queue once the hold has queued `hydrate_max_blobs`; that file is counted `too_many_blobs`, the blobs before it stay queued, the rest of the tree is not read (it is left to the reads), and the trees after it are `cut` with no request. A file that would newly queue more than what is left of `hydrate_max_bytes` is counted `too_many_bytes` and left out, the walk goes on to the files that fit and to the later trees, and its tree ends `cut`. A tree listed twice in a call, or one the hold is for already, is expanded once. For a small cache the `high_water` check when each blob's turn comes is the tighter bound.
- **Going back to the previous release needs an empty cache directory.** A blob the cache holds is one entry named by its digest, whichever path filled it, and an entry for a blob cut out of a pack (origin `span`) has that name too. The previous release does not know `origin` and serves such an entry as the object `cas/l/<sha256>`, so a client that decides by the object's presence whether to write it would skip the write. Empty `local_cache.path` before the rollback; the cost is a refill. The entries of the older layout (named `_tiering/fill/<sha256>`) are renamed at start, and nothing else of the directory changes.
- The queue and its places in the table are in memory: a restart forgets them. A blob already queued or running is joined, never queued twice, and a blob the cache holds costs no request.

The counters are in [metrics](metrics.md#the-prefetch-queue).

### Holds

A caller that queues blobs ahead of a job names the queueing a **hold** (not in a release yet; the hydrate call below is what opens a hold). A hold is a name, the trees it is for, and the blobs of those trees that it handed to the queue:

- A blob of a hold is **pinned** for the hold when it is in the cache: at once if the cache held it, else when the fill that brings it ends, whoever started that fill (a worker, or a read that took the blob from the queue). The pin is a `pin` of [the fill socket](operations.md#the-fill-socket) with an owner of its own, the mount `hold` and the tree `<hold>/<tree>`, so a blob of two trees or two holds is pinned for each and charged once, and `local_cache.pin_limit` bounds the pinned bytes as it does for the attachments of a mount: a blob past it is kept by recency only. A fill that ends without the blob, and a blob the queue drops when its turn comes (it waited too long, or does not fit), pin nothing.
- A hold ends when it is released, or at the time its caller gave, cut to `local_cache.hold_max` (default 4 hours), noticed at the cache's next tick (`local_cache.lock_interval`, one second unless set). Ending it takes away its pins and drops the blobs it queued that still wait for a worker and that no other hold waits for; a fill that has started is left to finish. What the hold asked for is told by the state of each blob: filled, present, dropped, failed, or still queued for another hold or filling.
- At most `local_cache.hold_max_count` holds are open (default 256). A hold opened again keeps its time and gains the trees, up to `local_cache.hydrate_max_trees` in all. A hold belongs to the key that opened it (the access key id): another key that names the id finds no hold, whether it adds trees or releases. The expansion of a hold's trees is one task per hold, which takes the trees the calls added in the order added and stops when the hold ends.
- **A hold hands its tree over to the job that attaches it.** The first `pin` of a mount for a tree that a hold lists moves every pin the hold has on that tree to the attachment (the pins of the mount and tree, which are in the pins file as every attachment's are), the blobs the request did not name included, and counts `tierstore_cache_hold_handovers_total`. A blob of the tree that is filled after that is pinned for each mount that has pinned for the tree, and not for the hold; so is every blob of a tree that a mount had pinned for before the hold asked for its first blob (no pin of the hold moved then, and no handover is counted). The `unpin` of a tree (the detach) and a `pins-live` that does not list it end the mount's part: a blob filled when no mount is left is kept by recency only. The release of the hold, or its end of time, takes away what the hold still owns and leaves the attachments' pins. The cache learns of an attachment from its pin requests, so a tree attached before a restart of the cache is known again at the mount's next pin.
- The pins of a hold are in memory only. They are not in the pins file, the `pins-live` request of the fill socket leaves them, and a restart forgets the holds and their pins: a pin whose hold is gone would never end.
- A read-only cache (one that does not hold the lock of its directory) opens no hold.

The gauge is in [metrics](metrics.md#the-prefetch-queue).

### The hydrate call

Experimental: the shape of the two calls may change until the measurement of the design says they stay. A caller that is about to hand the job scheduler a list of trees to mount sends the same list to the node's tierstore first, so that the blobs of the trees are in the cache, or on their way, when the job opens them. A job that opens a blob that is still being fetched waits for that fetch and takes no second one. Both calls are `POST`s on the S3 listener, signed like any request, to a path outside every bucket:

- `POST /_tierstore/v1/hydrate` with `{"hold": "<id>", "expires_ms": n, "trees": ["<sha256>", ...]}`. `hold` (1 to 128 bytes) and `trees` (64 lower-case hex digits each, the digests of root manifests) are required. `expires_ms` is optional: the hold ends by itself after that long, cut to `local_cache.hold_max` (the default is `hold_max`). A field the call does not know is ignored. The answer is `202` with `{"hold": "<id>", "accepted": n}`, `n` the distinct trees of the call, and is made as soon as the call is accepted: nothing is fetched before it, so the caller can send its job scheduler request at once. The cache then expands the trees in the order listed (see [the prefetch queue](operations.md#the-prefetch-queue)) and the prefetch workers fill the blobs, which the hold pins as they land (see [holds](operations.md#holds)). The same `hold` sent again by the same key adds its trees to the hold, up to `hydrate_max_trees` in all, and they are expanded after the trees of the earlier calls; a tree listed twice, or that an earlier call listed, is expanded once. A hold id is for one key: another key that sends it is answered as for a hold that is not open.
- `POST /_tierstore/v1/release` with `{"hold": "<id>"}` ends the hold: it takes away its pins, drops its blobs that still wait for a worker and that nothing else wants, and stops the expansion of its trees that is still running. A fill that has started is left to finish. The answer is `200` with `{"trees": n, "expanded": n, "cut": n, "refused": n, "blobs": {"filled": n, "present": n, "dropped": n, "failed": n, "pending": n}}`: the trees of the hold and how the expansion of each ended so far (`cut` is a walk that stopped at a bound of the call or at the end of the hold; `refused` includes a tree the key may not read), and what became of the blobs the hold asked for (`pending` are blobs still queued for another hold, or being filled). A hold that is not open, because it was released or its time passed, or that another key opened, is `404` `NoSuchHold`; the caller ignores it. The expansion of the hold stops at its next step: it asks for no manifest after the release. A hold the caller never releases ends at its time.

The answers that are not a success:

| Status and code | When |
|---|---|
| `400` `InvalidRequest` | the body is not the shape, the hold id is empty or over 128 bytes, or a tree is not 64 lower-case hex digits |
| `403` `AccessDenied` | a presigned URL, a presigning key, or a scoped key with no read prefix (see [keys](keys.md#the-keys-and-what-each-may-do)) |
| `404` `NoSuchHold` | a release of a hold that is not open, or a release or a hydrate of a hold that another key opened |
| `413` `EntityTooLarge` | more trees than `local_cache.hydrate_max_trees` in the call or, with the trees the hold has, in the hold, or a body larger than that many digests take; nothing is opened or read |
| `429` `TooManyRequests` | `local_cache.hold_max_count` other holds are open; the caller backs off |
| `501` `HydrateNotAvailable` | the node has no local cache, or `local_cache.prefetch_concurrency` is 0 |
| `503` `SlowDown` | the cache does not hold the lock of its directory (it is read-only, see [the cache lock](operations.md#the-cache-lock-and-read-only-mode)); the caller backs off |

A server of an earlier release does not know the path: it reads `/_tierstore/v1/hydrate` as a request to a bucket named `_tierstore` and answers a signed `POST` `404` `NoSuchBucket` (`501` `NotImplemented` under a real bucket), so a caller that reads a `404` or a `501` as "unsupported now", backs off, and asks again later finds a node that was updated. The call needs no write rights: a node that serves as a read cache (`write_through` off) takes it. Any error of the call costs the job speed and nothing else: the job reads through the cache as before. The counters are in [metrics](metrics.md#the-prefetch-queue).

### The virtual build file system

`tierstore fs --config <file>` is a process of its own, beside the build node's `tierstore`, that shows trees of content-addressed files as one FUSE mount (`fs.mountpoint`, default `/run/tierstore/fs`), for the jobs of the node. A tree is named by the SHA-256 of its root [tree manifest](tree-manifest.md); a client attaches it for a run, or the first lookup of `<mountpoint>/trees/<digest>` attaches it with no run, the job runtime gives `<mountpoint>/trees/<digest>` to the job (as the lower layer of the job's overlay, where the job writes), and the first open of each file fetches that file's bytes into the node's cache over [the fill socket](operations.md#the-fill-socket), so a job reads only what it opens. The design is [virtual-build-fs.md](https://github.com/komira-ai/tierstore/blob/main/docs/design/virtual-build-fs.md). The keys of the file are the `[fs]` table of [configuration](configuration.md#fs); it is not the file of the node's `tierstore`, and it holds no secret. The unit is `tierstore-fs@.service` (`packaging/hostkeeper/units`), which holds `CAP_SYS_ADMIN` alone, for the mount, and whose `RuntimeDirectory=` makes `/run/tierstore-fs` (the runtime directory) and `/run/tierstore` (the parent of the default mount point) for the account of the unit. It starts only under the marker `/var/lib/tierstore/run-fs-<instance>`. On a node that has no instance of the unit running, an operator creates the marker of instance `a` by hand after writing `/etc/tierstore/fs.toml`; from then on the update hook manages the unit: it hands the mount over to the other letter in an update between releases that both list `handoff-fs-v1`, and writes and removes the markers (see [the second unit](update-hook.md#the-second-unit-tierstore-fs)). The unit keeps the mount's descriptor in the service manager's store, so a crash or a restart does not unmount it (see [crash recovery and the hand-over of the mount](operations.md#crash-recovery-and-the-hand-over-of-the-mount)).

On a Mac the same process serves the trees over NFSv3 instead of FUSE: a loopback socket (`fs.nfs.listen`), mounted read-only at `fs.mountpoint` by the process itself as `fs.nfs.mount_user`, and unmounted before the process ends (the launchd job is `tierstore-fs`, `/Library/LaunchDaemons/tierstore-fs.plist`, which the macOS package installs and only the update hook loads; it runs the process as `_tierstore`, the store's account: the fill socket answers only a peer of its own user, so a process of any other user, root included, has every fill refused). How the mount ends when its server is gone, how the process unmounts, and how an update stops it are in the section "The Mac's mount" below. What follows holds for both except where it speaks of FUSE, passthrough or the kernel's keeping of answers: NFS file handles are the eight bytes of the inode number, four of the boot's epoch and fifty-two made from those twelve, sixty-four bytes in all, so a handle names the same entry after a restart in the same boot and is stale after a reboot (the fifty-two bytes spread the sums of the handles' bytes, by which the macOS NFS client finds the node of a handle, over thousands of values, where the twelve alone sum to a few hundred and every lookup of a node the client does not have walks a long chain; a handle of the twelve bytes alone, which a release before this one gave out, still names its entry, while a release before this one refuses the sixty-four bytes as a bad handle, so a hand-over over `fs.nfs.handoff_socket` back to such a release breaks the clients' handles; an update that unmounts first, as a Mac's does, makes a new mount whose handles are all new); a read of a cold blob waits for the fill and, past `fs.nfs.jukebox_after`, is told to be asked again. A restart of the process while the mount is up makes no second mount: the start finds the mount in the `mount` listing, reopens the table of tree numbers the earlier start of this boot left (so the clients' handles stay good), makes the attachments from the file again and serves on the same address (the address a mount was made from is noted in `nfs-mounted` in `fs.runtime_dir`, and a start that finds that mount and serves on another address unmounts it, by force if it is busy, and mounts afresh, since the mount would reach nothing and everything that touched it would wait; a start whose unmount fails is refused with what each step of the unmount said), and the clients' hard mount goes on after a pause. Requests are reads, so a request the client resends is answered as it was and no record of requests is kept. With `fs.nfs.handoff_socket` an update of the process hands the listening socket from the running process to the next over that socket: the running one stops accepting, answers the requests it has read, shuts down its side of each connection, waits up to `fs.nfs.drain_wait` for the clients to close theirs, and ends without unmounting; the clients connect again to the new process, which holds the same socket. The running process does not end when the next one has the socket: it ends when the next one says `ready` and it has answered `bye` (the next one serves only after it reads `bye`, so the two never serve together), which the next one does once its start is done (the table reopened, the attachments made again, the control socket bound) and it is about to serve; until then the running process goes on accepting and answering on the same socket, so the clients are served the whole time. From the moment the next process has the socket the running one changes nothing in the table of tree numbers or the attachments file: an attach or detach that reaches its control socket is refused with `this instance is being replaced by its successor: ask its control socket`, and the client asks again at the control socket path, which the next process binds in its start (and which is the running process's again if the hand-over fails, see below). If the next process is gone before it says `ready` (killed, or its start failed), sends anything else, or does not say it within `fs.nfs.handoff_ready_wait` of the running process's `done`, the hand-over has not happened: the running process lets its hold go, so that it takes attaches and detaches again, makes its hand-over socket again, binds its control socket again where the next process had replaced it, and serves on; the next update takes from it. A next process that was too slow finds its `ready` unanswered (the connection is closed) and exits with status 1 (raise `fs.nfs.handoff_ready_wait`). A running process of a release before the `ready` step ends at `done`, as it did, so the first update from such a release has no protection after `done`; the new process asks for the step and, refused as not understood, asks again without it, and a taker of such a release asks a new running process without it and is handed over as before. The next process checks its runtime directory, the table, the attachments file and the directory of its control socket before it asks for the socket, so a start that cannot work leaves the running process serving. The launchd jobs that alternate for it are a later change: until then the update hook restarts the Mac's process, unmounting first (see below). The settings are in [the fs.nfs table](configuration.md#fsnfs) of the configuration page.

What a process sees under the mount:

- The mount's root and `trees/` can be passed through (mode 0711) and cannot be listed (`EACCES`). `trees/<digest>` is there while some run has the tree attached. A lookup of a digest that no run has attached **resolves it**: the process reads the tree's manifests (the root `cas/l/<digest>` through the fill socket's `fill <digest>`, which checks the bytes against the digest and takes at most 64 MiB, where the cache does not hold them) and attaches the tree with no run, an **implicit attachment**. The manifests are fetched a level of the tree at a time, up to 16 at once, each level before the one below, so a tree whose manifests the cache does not hold costs about one fill for each level of the tree rather than one for each directory. Each such lookup is said on stderr with its time and where its manifests came from (`tierstore fs: lookup of <digest>: ok in 12 ms; 40 manifests: 38 from the cache, 2 filled, 0 not had`) and reported to the node for its [metrics](metrics.md#build-node-tree-attach-on-a-lookup). A digest whose root manifest the upstream does not have is `ENOENT`, asked of the upstream again at the next lookup; a tree that cannot be read otherwise (a manifest below the root missing or refused, the fill socket not answering) is `EIO`, said on stderr; neither takes a tree number. One read of a tree's manifests ends at most 30 seconds after it began, every fill it makes through the fill socket together, even with a longer `fs.fetch_timeout`: a job agent gives up on a lookup 60 seconds after it asked, and a lookup that took longer held one of the agent's few lookup threads in the kernel, so the lookups queued behind it timed out too. The same bound holds for the read of an attach and of a start that makes the attachments again. An implicit attachment ends when no request has named the tree or one of its inodes for `fs.tree_idle_secs` (default 900): unless a run has it, the tree is then unpinned and leaves memory at that look, whatever inodes of it the kernel holds; the next lookup attaches it again. Implicit attachments are in memory only: they are not in the `attachments` file or the `attachments` answer, a start has none (its `pins-live` drops their pins), and a lookup makes them again. While a hand-over is under way the running instance attaches nothing by lookup and detaches nothing for idleness: a lookup of a tree that has a tree number is answered with no attachment made, and one of a tree that has none is `EIO`, since only the next instance may number it. The answer for a name in `trees/` is never kept by the kernel, so an attach, a detach, or a tree the upstream gains is seen by the next lookup. Everything below a tree's root is immutable, and the kernel is told it may keep every entry, attribute, link target and absent name for as long as it keeps anything.
- The tree of a **placement** made by the node's trees API with an fs location appears at `<mountpoint>/<path>` of that location, and nowhere else (not under `trees/`). A lookup of a name in the mount's root asks the node's tierstore over the fill socket where the path is (`place`): the location of a placement is the root of its tree, which the process reads whole from the node's tree store, with no manifest: a tree of a version 2 tree list from its image in the cache directory (`image`), served in place with each entry's place in the list as its inode, and a tree of records flattened (`flat`, also what a node of the release before answers); it numbers the tree under the placement and the location (so one tree at two locations, of one placement or of two, is two trees of the mount, with inodes of their own), and attaches as a lookup of `trees/<digest>` does, implicitly, until it has been idle for `fs.tree_idle_secs`; the node holds the placement for the mount from the lookup until the tree of none of its locations is attached any longer (`unplace`). A path that a location is below (`jobs/` of `jobs/1f3a/root`) is a directory of mode 0711 that can be passed through and not listed, as the root is; any other name is `ENOENT`. Every such lookup is a request of the fill socket: while the socket is away (the node's `tierstore` is being updated, or does not hold the cache lock) it is asked again for up to `fs.fetch_timeout` and then answered `EIO` (a lookup of `trees/<digest>` that must read manifests is answered `EIO` within 30 seconds, see above). The kernel keeps none of these answers, so a placement that is released, or whose lease ends, stops resolving at the next lookup, while a job that has its files open keeps reading them. A file of a placed tree is a span of a pack in the cache: its open asks the node for the span (`span`), which is filled first if the cache does not hold it, and reads the pack's own cache file at the span's offset, as a cached open (never by passthrough, which would map the whole pack onto the file). Off a Mac the entries report their modes as stored, setuid, setgid and sticky included (the mount is `nosuid`, so they have no effect there); on a Mac see the modes below. A new process that takes the mount over reads a placed tree again by its placement, and asks the node for the locations (`mounted`) to find the path of a directory above them that it has not looked up.
- Every entry of a tree of manifests reports one owner, `fs.owner`, the mode 0644 (0755 for a file marked `exec` and for a directory, 0777 for a link) and one fixed modification time, 1790000000; the entries of a placed tree report the same owner and time, and their stored modes except on a Mac (NFS), where every mode is reported, and `ACCESS` answered, with the owner's read and execute given to group and other too, and without setuid, setgid and sticky: 0600 shows 0644, 0700 shows 0755, 0400 shows 0444, 4755 shows 0755, 0000 stays 0000, and no write bit is added (a job runs as a user of its own, never `fs.owner`, so an owner-only file would be unreadable to it; the export is read-only, and a tree is readable by anyone who knows its digest anyway). The mount's root, `trees/` and the directories above the fs locations of placements keep 0711. Every request that would change a tree is answered `EROFS`, and the extended attributes `ENOTSUP`. A job that writes does so in the overlay above the mount, never in the tree.
- A file's bytes are had by the first open of it: if the cache does not hold the blob, the process asks the node's tierstore to fetch it (from the pack the manifest names, or from the object `cas/l/<digest>` where it names none), waits for the answer, and opens the cache file. A fill that fails for a reason that can pass (the node's permits are busy, its upstream answered 5xx, the bytes were cut short, the node is being replaced, no space, no answer in `fs.fetch_timeout`, the cache file could not be opened) is asked again after a pause of a second, three times in all, while time is left: every try of one open is answered by one end, `fs.fetch_timeout` after the open began, and no pause is taken that would pass it, so an open waits for at most one `fs.fetch_timeout` (and its last open of the cache file) for its bytes, however many tries it makes. No such wait holds a reader of the connection: the reader answers at once an open whose bytes it can have without the node (the cache file of a blob pinned for its tree, or of a tree that is not attached; a file of a placed tree whose span the tree was given while the pack's file is still open; an empty file), and parks every other open, every read of a handle the process before gave out, and every lookup in the mount's root, `trees/` and the directories above the fs locations on one of `fs.threads` threads of their own, which answer them in turn; an open parked is answered by `fs.fetch_timeout` after it was parked, so one that waited out its time in the queue fails with `EIO` at once. A node that takes fills and does not answer them therefore holds those threads alone, and the mount goes on reading open files, opening the files the cache holds and answering every request of a tree in memory. A stop of the readers (a hand-over, a restart) waits until every parked request is answered. A cache file that cannot be opened for lack of descriptors (`EMFILE`, `ENFILE`) is not filled (the cache holds it) and not tried again: the open fails with `EIO` at once, recorded and said as `no descriptor: ` and what the system said, and counted in the node's `tierstore_fs_open_descriptor_failures_total` ([metrics](metrics.md#build-node-the-mounts-descriptors)); so is a fill that cannot connect to the fill socket for the same lack. A fill that still fails, or fails for a reason that does not pass (the upstream has no such object, the bytes do not hash to the digest), fails the open with `EIO`. Every attachment of the tree records the path, the digest and the words of the failure (`io: ` and what the system said for a cache file that could not be opened): a run's attachment answers it at the run's `detach`, and an implicit attachment says its failures on stderr when it ends (`tierstore fs: the attachment of the tree <digest> (number <n>) by a lookup ended with 2 failed opens: /a.h (<sha256>: mismatch); ...`). Each failed open is said on stderr at warn, with the tree, the path, the blob, where it is read from (the pack and offset, or `cas/l/<sha256>`), the last answer and the number of fills asked (`tierstore fs: cannot open /include/a.h in the tree <digest> (number 3): the blob <sha256> of 120 bytes from cas/b/.../pack @ 4096: upstream 404 (after 1 fills)`); the lines of one tree are said at most once every 10 seconds, each with the count of the failures since the line before, and a count that no later line carries is said on its own (`tierstore fs: 3 more failed opens of the tree <digest> (number 3) since the last line, not said`) at the next look for idle trees once the last line is 10 seconds old, or when the tree leaves memory. A read of a file that is open and that fails (the cache file cannot be read) is `EIO` and said the same way, with the tree, the path, the blob and the system's words (`tierstore fs: cannot read /include/a.h in the tree <digest> (number 3) at 4096: the blob <sha256> of 120 bytes from cas/l/<sha256>: Input/output error (os error 5)`), and so are the lookups and listings answered `EIO`. A read is never answered `ENOENT`: a read of a handle the process before gave out whose tree is gone (its placement ended while the mount was taken over) is `EIO`, said. A request for a tree that is gone (its root manifest is not upstream, or its placement has ended) is `ENOENT`; one for a tree that cannot be read now is `EIO`; both, and a table of tree numbers that cannot be read, are said on stderr at warn. A blob is pinned in the cache for the tree the first time it is opened under it while the tree is attached (by a run or a lookup), and unpinned when the tree's last attachment ends; an open of a tree that is not attached (its inodes are still held by a job after the end) pins nothing, so the blob is kept by recency alone. A cache file that is evicted while a job has it open stays readable. The handles of one cache file (a blob, or the pack of the spans of a placed tree) share one descriptor of it while the cache's name for it leads to the same file, so the process holds one descriptor for each such file however many handles there are: the kernel may release a file long after the job closed it (a job's files are opened through its overlay, whose lower layer is the mount), and a descriptor for each open of a build's headers ran out of the unit's `LimitNOFILE`, failing every open after it with `EIO`. The unit allows 1,048,576 open files (`LimitNOFILE=1048576`, as does the store's unit and, as `NumberOfFiles`, the launchd jobs), since a job's overlay keeps files of the mount open for as long as the job runs and the bound is the number of distinct cache files held open, not of handles. The process reports the cache files it holds open, and the opens that failed for lack of descriptors, to the node at each look for idle trees (every quarter of `fs.tree_idle_secs`, at least a second and at most a minute apart), for `tierstore_fs_cache_descriptors` and `tierstore_fs_open_descriptor_failures_total`. How the bytes then reach the job depends on the mode the mount runs in, which the process chooses at start from what the kernel offers (see the next bullet).
- **Cached opens.** Every open is a cached open (`FOPEN_KEEP_CACHE`): the kernel keeps the file's pages, and every read its page cache misses is answered by the process from the cache file, or from the span of the pack a file of a placed tree is. An empty file is a cached open too. A fill that fails fails the open with `EIO`. The process never asks the kernel for passthrough: the kernel would serve a passthrough file from a whole backing file at the file's own offsets, which cannot serve a span of a pack; and its first opens and last releases kept a table of ids under one lock that every open shared. With no passthrough the mount's stack depth is 0, and the job's overlay stacks on it. The start says what the kernel offers on stderr (`tierstore fs: kernel offers resend=...`). A connection whose `INIT` a release up to 0.3.121 answered with passthrough keeps that release's backing ids for as long as it lives (the service manager keeps its descriptor): a process that takes it over reads `<runtime dir>/backing` (one line `<sha256> <backing id> <opens>` for each blob with an id) once, writes it again with no count marked `exact` if one was, and answers every open of those blobs with their ids, since the kernel fails with `EIO` a cached open of an inode that has a passthrough open; it registers no new id and closes none. A start that mounts afresh removes the file, whose ids belonged to a connection that is gone.
- The cache directory (`fs.cache_path`) must not be on a stacked file system (overlayfs, eCryptfs): the job's overlay above the mount would pass the kernel's depth of stacking. A start on one is refused with status 3, naming the file system.

The control socket (`fs.control_socket`, mode 0660, group `fs.client_group`) takes one line and answers one line of JSON, in order on a connection:

- `attach <sha256> for <run>`, or `attach <sha256> for <run> prefetch all`: reads and flattens the tree (its manifests are fetched through the fill socket where the cache does not hold them), and answers `{"ok":true,"path":"<mountpoint>/trees/<sha256>"}` or `{"ok":false,"error":"..."}` with the refusal by name (`not a sha256 digest`, `not a usable run name`, the manifest's refusal such as `unsupported entry kind`, `tree too large` or `cannot fetch manifest ...: upstream 404`). A run is one word of at most 128 visible ASCII characters. Attaching a tree twice for one run is one attachment. With `prefetch all` the answer is the same and does not wait for the rest: a thread then fills every non-empty blob of the tree that the cache does not hold, one at a time on the fill socket's permits, in the order of the manifest (depth first, children by name), and pins them for the tree in requests of at most 512 blobs. It stops at the next blob when the tree is no longer attached (no run has it and no lookup attached it), and sends no pin after the tree is unpinned. A blob that cannot be had is recorded on the attachment as a failed open records it and the others go on. A tree being prefetched is not prefetched again by a second attach.
- `detach <run>`: ends every attachment of the run and answers `{"ok":true,"failures":[{"path":"/bin/cc","digest":"...","reason":"upstream 404"}]}`, the files whose open failed (once for each path), or `{"ok":false,"error":"not attached"}`. A runtime that gets a failure marks the run as an infrastructure failure, whatever the job's exit status.
- `detach-all-except [<run> ...]`: ends the attachments of every run not listed and answers `{"ok":true,"detached":<runs>}`; a client that restarts uses it to say which runs are live.
- `attachments`: `{"ok":true,"attachments":[{"tree":"...","run":"..."}]}`.

The attachments of runs are kept in `attachments` in the runtime directory (`fs.runtime_dir`, the unit's `RuntimeDirectory=`), one line `<digest> <run>` for each (the implicit attachments of lookups are not), and made again at the next start, before the process serves or answers on its control socket: the manifests of the trees are read (fetched through the fill socket where the cache does not hold them) up to `fs.reattach_concurrency` trees at once, each tree once however many runs hold it, so a start takes about the longest few fills and not the sum of them (cold, a start of 66 attachments waited for each tree's manifests in turn; the serial figures were 3.4 s warm and about 2 minutes cold), and the result does not depend on which read ends first. The start waits for every tree before it serves, since a path a job was given must not answer `ENOENT` while its tree is still being read. It writes the file once when it is done; a tree whose manifests cannot be had then is not served and is said on stderr, but its line stays in the file until its run attaches again or is detached (`detach` and `detach-all-except` drop it). An attach whose file cannot be written takes back that attachment alone, and an attach and a detach never run together, so a detach cannot remove the pins of an attach that follows it. `tierstore fs attachments --config <file>` lists them for an operator, one `<digest> <run>` line each. The inode numbers of a tree are `tree_number << 24 | index`, the index being the entry's place in the tree flattened depth first in manifest order, and the tree numbers are kept in `trees` in the runtime directory (header `layout 1`, then 32 bytes for each tree), written before the first reply that names an inode of the tree; a tree whose last attachment has ended leaves memory when the kernel has forgotten its inodes, or, at the latest, at the first look for idle trees after no request has named it for `fs.tree_idle_secs`, whatever inodes of it the kernel still holds (the kernel forgets inodes only when it is short of memory, so a tree kept until then could stay for as long as the process lives); it is read again if a request names one, and the answer is the same while the tree can be read (a tree gone by then, its root manifest no longer upstream or its placement ended, is `ENOENT`, as after a restart). The trees in memory are thus those attached, by a run or a lookup, and those detached that a request named within `fs.tree_idle_secs` and a look (a minute at most): with the default, the trees of the last 16 minutes of jobs beyond those attached. The read is made once: the requests that name the tree while it is read wait for that read and are answered from it. A forget of more lookups than the process counted (the kernel keeps its inodes across a restart or a hand-over, and the counts start again at zero; or the tree was read again after it left memory) keeps the tree in memory until nothing has it attached and no request has named it for `fs.tree_idle_secs`. At each look for idle trees the process says on stderr at info, when they changed since the line before, the trees in memory, how many of them are attached, their entries and an estimate of the bytes they hold, rounded up to a MiB (`tierstore fs: 12 trees in memory, 3 of them attached: 412000 entries, about 46 MiB`); the estimate counts each tree's entries, names and tables, not what the allocator adds. A read of a tree after its first in the process, or one that took a second or more, is said on stderr with its entries, its time and the requests that waited for it (`tierstore fs: read the tree <digest> (number <n>, read <k> times in this process): ...`). A read that failed, the first too, is said at warn, once every 10 seconds for a tree, with the count of the failed reads of the tree since the line before.

A start says each of its steps on stderr with its time as the step ends (`tierstore fs: <step> took <n> ms`: the directories, the core and the attachments made again, the put directory, the mount or the connection taken over), and the time from its start until it tells the service manager it is ready (`tierstore fs: ready after <n> ms`). It does not wait for the fill socket to be ready: the pins of the mount are told to the node from a thread once the socket answers (`tierstore fs: the fill socket has the mount's pins and placements`; while it does not, `tierstore fs: the fill socket does not have the mount's pins yet (...); asking again`).

The process exits 0 on SIGTERM or SIGINT; a start that cannot be done exits 1 (2 for a file that is refused, 3 for a cache on a stacked file system). It says `READY=1` to the service manager's `NOTIFY_SOCKET` once the mount is served. Where there is a service manager (`NOTIFY_SOCKET` is set), SIGTERM stops the readers, each having answered the request it held, and **leaves the mount** for the descriptor store to keep (see below); started by hand, with no service manager, it unmounts.

### The Mac's mount: a dead server, a stop and an update

The Mac's mount is `hard` with `deadtimeout` (`fs.nfs.dead_timeout`, by default `fs.fetch_timeout` and 30 s, so 90 s): a server that restarts is waited for, as before, and a mount whose server is gone ends by itself instead of making everything that touches it wait.

What the manual and the source say:

- `mount_nfs(8)`: "If the server becomes unresponsive while an NFS file system is mounted, any new or outstanding file operations on that file system will hang uninterruptibly until the server comes back (or that NFS file system is forcibly unmounted)."
- `mount_nfs(8)`, `deadtimeout=<timeout>`: "If the mount is still unresponsive timeout seconds after it is initially reported unresponsive, then mark the mount as dead so that it will be forcibly unmounted." Only a mount that is both `soft` and read-only gets a default (60 s); a `hard` mount has none, so it is never marked dead unless the option is given.
- `mount_nfs(8)`, `mutejukebox`: requests that repeatedly get `NFS3ERR_JUKEBOX` make the mount count as unresponsive. The server answers a read that waits for a fill with `NFS3ERR_JUKEBOX` after `fs.nfs.jukebox_after`, again and again until the fill ends, at most `fs.fetch_timeout`; that is why `fs.nfs.dead_timeout` must be more than `fs.fetch_timeout`, and the configuration refuses one that is not.
- Apple's NFS client source (apple-oss-distributions/NFS, tag NFS-343.100.5): the check of the dead timeout (`nfs_mount_check_dead_timeout`, `kext/nfs_socket.c`) looks at the timeout alone, not at `hard`, `soft` or read-only; a call on a mount marked dead fails with `ENXIO`; while a mount with a dead timeout is unresponsive, `statfs` is answered from the cache instead of waiting (`nfs_use_cache`); and the kernel marks the mount dead and asks for it to be unmounted (`VQ_DEAD`), it does not unmount it itself.

What only a run on a Mac shows (not measured yet): that a reader of the mount gets an error within the dead timeout and a margin after its server is killed, which error it is (`ENXIO` by the source), that the mount then leaves the `mount` listing, and that the rest of the Mac (launchd, the network, the walkers of the mounts) stays responsive meanwhile.

What a job sees when its tree mount dies: its reads of the trees fail, so the job fails; nothing waits for ever. The next start of `tierstore fs` mounts afresh.

**The stop.** The process unmounts before it stops serving, on every way out it controls: SIGTERM or SIGINT (a launchd bootout), a start that fails after it made the mount, and a server that stops by itself (its accept failed, or it panicked). It ends the offer of its listening socket (so no successor takes the mount meanwhile) and, while it still answers the clients, unmounts: `umount`, then `diskutil unmount force`, then its own `unmount(2)` with `MNT_FORCE`, which runs no program at all. Each step is bounded by `fs.nfs.unmount_wait` (20 s; a command still running then is killed with its process group), says what it found on stderr in a line (`tierstore fs: /sbin/umount <path>: Resource busy`, `... unmounted`), and an unmount that needed force says so once (`tierstore fs: umount of <path> failed (...); unmounted by diskutil unmount force, ...`). The whole stop is at most four such steps, well inside the job's `ExitTimeOut` (150 s). If every step fails the process does not exit: it says why (`tierstore fs: unmounting <path>: <what each step said>; the process goes on serving the mount and tries again in 10s`), serves on and tries again. If launchd kills it then, the dead timeout ends the mount. A hand-over ends without unmounting: the mount is the successor's. `umount` and `diskutil` may be refused to a process that launchd started where they work from an administrator's shell; the `unmount(2)` of the process itself is the step that does not depend on that (not measured yet).

**An update.** The update hook manages the fs job by the param `fs_launchd_label` (`tierstore-fs`, which the macOS package's manifest carries; see [the update hook](update-hook.md)). At a cutover it boots the fs job out first and waits until it is gone (its stop unmounts), then checks the `mount` listing: a mount that is still there refuses the cutover (`not_ready`, naming the mount point), the main job is not touched, and the fs job is started again so the mount has a server. Otherwise it updates the main job, and starts the fs job after it. A rollback, a restore and an uninstall do the same.

**The first update from a hand-installed fs job.** A job installed by hand before the package shipped one runs a release whose stop unmounts with `umount` and `diskutil` only, which may not return under launchd: the hook cannot make that stop safe. So before the first update that installs this package, with no job running on the Mac:

1. From an administrator's shell, while the old fs process still serves: `sudo diskutil unmount force /private/var/tierstore/fs`.
2. Check that `mount | grep ' on /private/var/tierstore/fs ('` prints nothing. If it still prints the mount, stop: do not update.
3. Let the update run. The hook boots the old fs job out (its stop finds nothing mounted and ends at once), finds no mount, updates the main job and starts the package's fs job, which mounts afresh.

The hand-installed `/usr/local/tierstore/etc/fs.toml` keeps working (a numeric `fs.owner` is still taken); `packaging/macos/fs.toml` is the file to install on a new Mac.

### Crash recovery and the hand-over of the mount

The mount outlives the process. The kernel keeps a FUSE connection until the last file of `/dev/fuse` for it is closed, so the process gives its descriptor to the service manager (`FDSTORE=1`, `FDNAME=fuse`, with `BARRIER=1` to know the manager has it before `READY=1`), and the unit holds it (`FileDescriptorStoreMax=1`, `FileDescriptorStorePreserve=restart`). A process that is killed, crashes or is restarted by `systemctl restart` leaves the mount where it was; requests that come meanwhile wait in the kernel, and jobs block in `open` and `lookup`. systemd restarts the unit (`Restart=on-failure`) and passes the descriptor back (`LISTEN_PID`, `LISTEN_FDS`, `LISTEN_FDNAMES`, the name `fuse`), and the new process serves it:

- It makes `fs.runtime_dir` and checks the cache directory, and does nothing at `fs.mountpoint`: that is the live mount, whose root is not cached by the kernel, so a `stat` of it is a request that waits for this process's readers, which have not started. It reads what the process before left in `fs.runtime_dir`: the table of tree numbers (`trees`), the attachments (`attachments`), the backing table (`backing`) and the record of the kernel's `INIT` handshake (`init`: the flags, `max_write`, `max_readahead`, `time_gran`, `max_stack_depth` and the protocol version the kernel and the process agreed on, the id the pins are made under, and a count of the processes that have served the connection). The kernel sends `INIT` once per mount, so the new process serves the descriptor from that record, with no handshake. A descriptor whose state cannot be read (no record, a table of another layout) is not served: it is taken out of the store (`FDSTOREREMOVE=1`), its connection is aborted so that nothing hangs on it, the mount is detached, and the mount is made afresh.
- Where the kernel offers `FUSE_HAS_RESEND` (Linux 6.9 and later; the process asks for it in `INIT`), the new process sends `FUSE_NOTIFY_RESEND`, and every request the process before had read and not answered is queued again, with the high bit of its id set, and answered: a request that was waiting on a slow fill is served about 30 ms after the start (Measured: linux E7). Every request on a tree is idempotent.
- Where it does not (Linux 6.8, the lab's today), requests the dead process had read stay on the connection, unanswered, and the jobs that issued them hang. The bound is `fs.recover_wait`: the new process looks at `/sys/fs/fuse/connections/<n>/waiting` every 100 ms, `<n>` being the minor number of the mount's device in `/proc/self/mountinfo`; a look that finds no request waiting ends the watch, and none for the whole of `fs.recover_wait` writes `1` to `abort` there, which fails every request that waits (an I/O error, which the job's runtime tells from a build error by the attachment's record), takes the descriptor out of the store and ends the process with status 1, so that the unit starts again and mounts afresh. A connection that stays too busy to show a moment with nothing waiting for `fs.recover_wait` is aborted too, which is why it is a setting. Where the kernel does not show the file (a kernel without `fusectl` mounted, or a process not allowed to read it), nothing is aborted and the process says so.
- After that abort, every overlay a runtime mounted over the old connection is dead, not only the job that had the request waiting: its opens, reads of pages not yet cached and `stat`s fail with `ENOTCONN` (a read of pages cached before the abort still succeeds, and a read that crosses into uncached pages returns a short count and then `ENOTCONN`). The unit's start again mounts a new connection; the runtime must mount its overlays again over it, and a job on an old overlay must be failed or run again.
- A file a job had open across the restart keeps working. The file handles the process before gave out are told from this process's by their number (a count of the processes that have served the connection, kept in `init`): the first read that names one makes the cached open again from its inode, and its release ends that open. The backing ids an earlier release registered stay registered with the kernel until the connection ends; the new process takes them from `backing`, as above.
- When the unit gives up restarting (`StartLimitBurst=5` within `StartLimitIntervalSec=60`) systemd drops the store, the last file of the connection is closed, and the kernel aborts it: jobs fail instead of hanging, and the next start mounts afresh: a mount of this file system (type `fuse.tierstore-fs`) that `/proc/self/mountinfo` shows at the mount point has no process serving it by then, and is aborted and detached first. The start finds it in the table of mounts and does not look at the mount point, since a `stat` of a mount whose descriptor a process holds and does not read waits for ever; a mount of another file system there is left. `systemctl stop` does the same.

**The hand-over** (`handoff-fs-v1`, listed by `tierstore --capabilities`) moves the mount from the running instance to a new one of the other letter with no moment in which nobody holds it, as the listening sockets of the S3 process are handed over (`server.handoff_socket`). A new instance asks on `fs.handoff_socket` with `take-fs 1` (and a second line, `ready`, which a release before this one refuses; the new instance then asks with `take-fs 1` alone); it refuses a running instance of another user. The running instance offers the `/dev/fuse` descriptor and the listening control socket (`SCM_RIGHTS`) with the mount point and the control socket path it serves, which must be the new instance's own settings (otherwise it answers `nak` and the running instance goes on); the new instance puts the descriptor in its unit's store and waits until the manager has it, reads the state, and says `ack`; the running instance stops accepting on the control socket (a client that connects meanwhile waits in its backlog; a request on a connection that is open is answered `{"ok":false,"error":"handed over"}`, which ends it), has each reader answer the request it holds and stop, waits for the requests it parked (opens waiting on a fill, by `fs.fetch_timeout`) and for the commits of the put directory still running (an upload by `fs.put.commit_deadline_secs`; a commit waiting for the release of its file answers `EBUSY` at once, as that release now goes to the new instance), writes the backing table with exact counts and says `done`; the new instance, which checked that the device is the FUSE device and the control socket listens before its `ack`, reads its state and prepares the session without reading the device, and says `ready`; the running instance removes the descriptor's entry from its unit's store, answers `bye` and ends with status 0 at once, leaving its memory and open files to the end of the process rather than freeing them one by one; only after the `bye` does the new instance read the device and accept on the control socket. A commit that waits longer than `fs.handoff_wait` makes the hand-over fail, and the running instance serves on: keep `fs.put.commit_deadline_secs` below `fs.handoff_wait`. Requests that came between `done` and the first read wait in the kernel and are served by the new instance; none is answered twice, and none needs `FUSE_NOTIFY_RESEND`, so the hand-over works on Linux 6.8. A new instance that fails before `ready`, or does not say it within `fs.handoff_wait` of `done` (the wait covers its whole start, including the attachments it makes again; set the key above that time on a node with many attached trees), has not taken the mount: the running instance reads requests again, accepts again and binds its socket again. A peer of another user is refused.

The hook (see [the update hook](update-hook.md#the-second-unit-tierstore-fs)) hands the mount over only when both releases list `handoff-fs-v1`; with another pair it leaves `tierstore fs` running on its release and updates the S3 process alone, since a restart of the process on a kernel without `FUSE_HAS_RESEND` (before Linux 6.9) can leave requests unanswered until `fs.recover_wait` aborts the connection, and that fails jobs. It checks no layout of the runtime directory: the stored formats are additive and each release reads the other's, and a release that changes one in a way the other cannot read stops listing `handoff-fs-v1` for a new word. The stored formats, all additive, are in [stored formats](https://github.com/komira-ai/tierstore/blob/main/docs/stored-formats.md).

### The `fs` interface: the mount in the node's own process

On Linux a build node whose file has an `[[interface]]` of type `fs` (see [configuration](configuration.md#the-fs-interface)) serves the mount itself, in the same process as its S3 front, with the start and the stop of `tierstore fs` above; its file system calls the node's cache and tree store instead of asking the fill socket. The start, in order:

1. It reads and checks the file, and opens the cache and the tree store.
2. It gets the mount. A running instance that answers `take-fs 1` on `handoff.sock` in `params.runtime_dir` gives it: a `tierstore fs` process of the two-process layout, or a process of this layout, which answers there too. Else the descriptor the service manager kept under the name `fuse` is served. Else the mount is made afresh, as above. A running instance that accepts on the socket and does not answer fails the start after `params.handoff_wait`, before anything is detached or mounted; the start never takes a mount that answers for one that is dead.
3. The mount is served (the instance it was taken from is told `ready`, and ends if it is a `tierstore fs` process), and the start answers on `handoff.sock` itself. Only then does it bind the trees front and take or bind the S3 and OCI sockets, on `server.handoff_socket` as any build node does, so that a start that cannot get the mount leaves the running instance serving everything.
4. It tells the service manager `READY=1`, as every build node's start now does where the manager listens (`NOTIFY_SOCKET`).

So the switch of a node from two processes to one is a start of the node's `tierstore` on a file with the `fs` interface while both old processes run: it takes the mount from `tierstore fs` and the sockets from the old `tierstore`. A process of this layout whose mount a successor took serves S3 and OCI until the successor takes their sockets too, then drains and exits 0. Until the cache's lock is handed over with them (a later release), the successor's cache is read-only until the process it took them from has exited, and a fill of the file system waits for the write role up to `params.fetch_timeout`, as a fill that finds no fill socket does today.

The stop: at SIGTERM or SIGINT the fronts drain first, and the mount is served throughout, since a fill a job waits for may need the upstream; once they have drained, the readers stop and the mount is left to the service manager's store, as `tierstore fs` leaves it (a process with no manager unmounts). A mount that cannot be served on (a restart's connection aborted after `params.recover_wait`, or a hand-over that left the readers stopped) stops the fronts as a signal does, and the process exits 1 so that the unit starts again. A cache directory on a stacked file system is exit status 3, as for `tierstore fs`.

## Keys under a tree placement

A build node can show a node-local tree as objects of its bucket: a placement of the tree at a bucket and a key prefix, kept by the node's tree store (see the `[trees]` table in [configuration](configuration.md#trees)). The `CreateTree` call of the `trees` gRPC interface (a `trees` entry of `[[interface]]`, see [configuration](configuration.md#interface)) is the front that makes placements; until that front is connected to the tree store, its calls answer `NotServing`. While a placement lasts (until its lease ends or it is released), a key under its prefix is a file of the tree on this node, not an ordinary object. Another node does not see the placement: it answers the same keys from its own objects.

- **Reading.** `GET` and `HEAD` of a file answer as for an object whose bytes are the file's. The length is the file's size, the `ETag` is its SHA-256 as quoted hex, `Last-Modified` is the time the placement was made, and `x-amz-meta-mode` is its mode in four octal digits (`4755`). A `Range`, the conditions (`If-Match` and the others) and the `response-*` overrides work as for an object. The bytes come from the cache; the first read that does not find them there fills them from the upstream, whole and checked against the file's SHA-256.
- **Symbolic links** are objects of no bytes. Their `ETag` is the SHA-256 of nothing, and `x-amz-meta-symlink-target` holds the target, with each byte that is not printable ASCII, and `%`, written `%XX`.
- **Directories are not objects.** A directory, the root of the placement included, and a path that is not in the tree answer `404 NoSuchKey`.
- **Writes are refused.** A `PUT`, an upload part, the start or the completion of a multipart upload and a `DELETE` of a key under a placement answer `409 TreeLocationReadOnly`. The abort of a multipart upload is not refused, since it changes no object. A DeleteObjects call refuses each such key alone, with the same code and message in its `Error` entry, and deletes the others.
- **Listings.** A listing (`ListObjectsV2`, `ListObjects` and the version listing) merges the files and links of the placements it meets with the ordinary objects, in key order, which is the order of the bytes of the keys, not of the names in the tree: a file `a-b` comes before the keys `a/...`, since `-` sorts before `/`. An ordinary object whose key is under a placement is left out, because the tree answers that key on this node. A directory is not a key, so an empty directory shows nothing.
- **A placement that ends** between the lookup of a request and its answer makes the key an ordinary one again, and the request is answered as for an ordinary key.
- **A fill that fails.** When the bytes of a file are not in the cache and cannot be filled, the answer is `503 SlowDown` with `retry-after: 1` if a retry can mend it (the cache is busy, read-only or full, the entry changed under the fill, or the upstream answered a server error), and `500 InternalError` if the source does not hold the file's bytes. A client retries the first and not the second.

## The mount provider

A build node with a `mount-provider` entry of `[[interface]]` (see [configuration](configuration.md#interface)) serves jobscheduler's mount provider protocol (`jobscheduler.provider.v1.MountProvider`, gRPC over the Unix socket of `params.socket`). jobscheduler's agent on the node is given the socket (`--mount-provider tierstore=<socket>`) and the mount of `tierstore fs` as a read-only mount root, and it calls the provider for a job whose layer or mount names `{provider: "tierstore", ref: <key>}`. The ref is the key of a tree list (`cas/l/<sha256 of its bytes>`, the format is in [the tree manifest page](tree-manifest.md#the-tree-list): version 1, JSON Lines, or [version 2](tree-manifest.md#the-tree-list-version-2), binary), which the client wrote to the store before it submitted the job.

- `Prepare` at the job's assignment: the provider checks the ref and the mode and answers at once. A ref that is not the key of a tree list is `INVALID_ARGUMENT`; a mode other than read-only (the trees are read-only) and a source prepared before with another ref are `FAILED_PRECONDITION`; a source prepared before with the same ref is a success. In the background it reads the list through the cache, checks that it hashes to its key, makes the tree of exactly its entries (a version 2 list is kept as the tree itself, named by its own digest: no directory record is made) and places it in the tree store at `<mountpoint>/mount-provider/<agent>/<run>/<index>` (the agent and the run in hex), where `tierstore fs` shows it. It reads every file's bytes into the cache, at most `trees.fill_concurrency` reads of the node at once, a file alone with the fill an open of the mount makes for that file: an open of a file still being read waits for that read, and nothing is read twice (files of one digest are read once, at the span the tree keeps for it; in a tree of a version 2 list each file is read at its own span, and a span several files have once). For a version 2 list the reads begin as soon as the list is walked, while the list is still being hashed and written to the tree's directory, and the tree is placed once the hash is the key's; the files whose spans lie in one of the list's pack ranges are read together, one read of the pack across them (at most 8 MiB) with each file checked against its own digest. For a version 1 list, the reads begin once the tree is placed, each file alone. If the list is refused, or the source released, the reads not yet begun are not made. With `trees.prepare_prefetch = "none"` (see [configuration](configuration.md#trees)) the provider makes none of these reads: it reads the list, makes and places the tree, and reads no file and pins nothing, so each file is read into the cache when a job first opens it (an open of a file another open is filling waits for that fill); `Mount`, `Release` and `Sweep` are the same. The default is `"background"`, the reads above. The log says the mode once when the provider starts (`trees.prepare_prefetch = "none": Prepare reads no file: each is read when first opened`).
- `Mount` answers the directory once the tree is placed (its files may still be filling), or `UNAVAILABLE` `not ready` after 25 seconds, and the agent asks again. A ref the provider cannot use is `FAILED_PRECONDITION` with the reason: no object at the key, a list the reader refuses (naming the line, or for version 2 the entry, key or range) or over 256 MiB, a file without its `sha256`, or a tree the store refuses (too large, or a path both a file and a directory). A failure that may pass is `UNAVAILABLE` with the reason, and the next `Mount` or `Prepare` begins the preparation again: the store did not answer within `params.fetch_timeout`, the node holds as many placements as `trees.max_placements`, or the object's bytes do not hash to its key (the cache refuses such bytes as it refuses any bad read of the store). A source whose placement's lease ended is made again by the next `Mount`, which answers `UNAVAILABLE` meanwhile.
- `Release` at the end of the job stops the reads and removes the placement: the path stops resolving, the tree goes when nothing holds it, and its packs stay in the cache to be evicted in their turn. A source that is not known is a success.
- `Sweep` after a start of the agent releases every source of that agent whose run the agent did not adopt, including the placements this process finds under the agent's path after a restart of its own.

A placement lasts `trees.ttl_max` (24 hours by default) from its preparation unless it is released, so a source an agent never released goes in its turn. Every reason is at most 256 bytes. The socket is bound, mode 0600, only while the cache holds the lock of its directory, as the fill socket is: in a hand-over of the S3 instance the old one answers until it exits and the new one binds when it takes the lock; the agent asks again when it finds the socket closed. The agent runs as root and connects to the socket of the user the node runs as.

On a Mac the socket is `/usr/local/tierstore/run/provider.sock` and the mount `/private/var/tierstore/fs` ([the Mac's file](https://github.com/komira-ai/tierstore/blob/main/packaging/macos/tierstore.toml)).

## The put ingest

A Mac has no put directory of `tierstore fs`: a job's put directory there is a plain directory on the local disk, and a build node with `local_cache.put_ingest` (see [configuration](configuration.md#local_cache)) stores what the jobs commit into it. The root is the key's directory (on a Mac `/usr/local/tierstore/put`, made by the installer package, owned by the user the node runs as, mode 0755), and a job's put directory is `<root>/cas/b/<build>/<nonce>`. A job commits an object there as the job's task program commits into the put directory of Linux, with no change: it writes `pack.part`, renames it to `pack.sha256-<64 hex>.part`, which declares the SHA-256 the object's bytes must have, and renames that to `pack`. The node watches the root (FSEvents on macOS, inotify on Linux), sees the declaring name as it passes, and when it finds the committed name it puts the file's bytes, read once and hashed as they are sent, as the create-only object `cas/b/<build>/<nonce>/pack` that names that digest: the put the fill socket's `put` makes for a commit of the Linux put directory, through the cache, so the cache keeps a copy under a prefix it keeps. Then it removes the file and, when it is empty, the job's directory. A job's object is in the store a moment after its commit rename returns, normally before the job has ended; a reader that finds the key missing asks again.

- Stored: a regular file with one link, directly in a job's directory, under a name without `.part`, whose digest was declared. Its key is its path under the root. A key that holds the same bytes already is a success, as a put sent again.
- Refused, nothing stored and the file removed (said on stderr at warn, `the put ingest refused <path>: <why>`): bytes that are not the declared digest (a truncated, longer or changed file), a key that holds other bytes, a size the upstream refuses, a symbolic link, a hard link, a file that is not regular, and a committed file whose digest was not declared within 60 seconds of its first finding (one committed while no instance watched the root, or whose events were lost). Its job's result is then missing, and its run fails as the farm's and is tried again.
- Left and tried again at the next scan: a file the node cannot open or read (said at warn, `cannot read`), and a put the upstream did not take (`failed, left`).
- Never stored: a `.part` name. A job's directory unchanged for 24 hours (a job that ended without a commit) is removed with what it holds, and a build's directory left empty for 24 hours too.

A whole scan of the root runs at the start, every 30 seconds, and whenever the watcher says it lost events or a directory appeared; it finds what no event led to and tries the puts that were left. At most 8 puts run at once. Only the process that holds the cache lock ingests: in a restart the new process takes the files left with its first scan, and the declarations of files committed while no process watched are lost, so those are refused. At the halt the puts that are running finish, and none is started.

What the job runtime does (jobscheduler's agent, which runs as root): it lists the root as the mount root of the jobs' put directories; before a job starts it makes the missing `cas`, `cas/b` and `cas/b/<build>` with the root's owner, group and mode, and `<nonce>` owned by the job's user, group the root's group (`_tierstore` on a Mac), mode 0770, and mounts it read-write at the job's put target; the files the job makes there must be readable by that group (a umask that leaves group read, 022 or 027, or an inheritable ACL entry for the group on `<nonce>`). It never removes the put directory or what is in it: the node does, and a removal before the node stored the object loses it. A file the node cannot read is left, said, and tried again at each scan until it can, or until its directory is abandoned.

## A storage node with a local cache

A file with `[hot_store]`, `[cold_store]` and `[local_cache]` (see [configuration](configuration.md#a-file-for-a-storage-node-with-a-cache)) runs one process that owns the data and keeps a disk cache in front of its own proxy: the S3 front and the OCI front read through the cache, and the cache reads from the proxy. What the cache keeps is as for a build node: a key under a `[[policy]]` with `cache = true`. A hit is answered from the cache directory with no call to either tier (the hot tier is skipped); a miss reads through the proxy, which reads the hot tier and, for an object only the cold tier has, the cold tier. The cache fills with ranged reads, and a ranged read of an object only the cold tier has is served from the cold tier and not copied up; the copy-up described above is for proxy reads without a range.

Writes pass through the cache to the proxy, so its entries stay right: a create-only put goes to the proxy, which answers `AlreadyExists` for a key either tier has; a plain put goes to the proxy as its plain put, which replaces a key on `active-marks` and drops the cache's older copy of it; and under a `cache = true` prefix the object is also kept; a presigned put goes to the proxy as a hot-only put, so the one-winner rule holds; a delete drops the cache's entry, deletes through the proxy (both tiers and the journal) and drops the entry again; a multipart upload passes to the proxy and is not kept (the first read fills it). The cache's versions of an object are compared by size while it fills, because an object's tag is the tag of the tier that answers and moves when the proxy copies it between tiers; the check of an old hit (`local_cache.revalidate_after`) compares the tag as well, and reads the object again if the tier that answers now tags it differently.

The start opens the proxy, then opens the cache directory: its own lock, the file-system probe if this process holds the lock, and the budget against the file system less `free_floor`. The cache has its own lock, `<path>/.lock`, beside the reconciler's `lock_file`; a process started beside a draining one serves at once from the proxy, its cache is `ready read-only` until the holder exits (see [the cache lock](operations.md#the-cache-lock-and-read-only-mode)), and its reconciler waits for the lock file or the lease. Put the cache path in a directory of its own, not inside either tier's directory, and, with a `dir` hot tier, on another file system: the cache then does not duplicate the hot tier's blocks on one disk, and its `free_floor` eviction does not react to the hot tier filling it. The loader checks the paths by component, with `.` and `..` resolved by text and not through links; the cache path and the directory of a `dir` tier must be absolute.

The delete and one-winner guarantees of a delete or a put that races a copy-back depend on the fixes to the proxy and the reconciler that make a delete conditional on what it found. They hold once every process over the tiers runs a version that has those fixes; while processes of different versions share the tiers, they do not.

## Turning the hot tier off

A node with `[hot_store]`, `[cold_store]` and `[local_cache]` becomes a node with the cache and the cold tier alone (see [configure](configure.md#a-node-that-owns-the-data-with-a-cache-and-the-cold-tier)) when `[hot_store]` is removed from its file and it is restarted by the hand-off: the new instance takes the sockets, the old one drains, gives up the reconciler's lease and its `hot_store.lock_file`, and exits. A storage node with no cache moves the same way to a file with `[cold_store]` alone (see [configure](configure.md#a-front-over-the-cold-tier-alone)): the new instance takes the same sockets and serves the cold store directly.

The hot tier can hold what the cold tier does not, and a node without it reads the cold tier only:

- an object written through a process with the hot tier, until the reconciler has copied it (its marker in the marks bucket says `DIRTY`);
- a delete the cold tier has not had yet (a marker with `gone` true): the cold copy is still there, and a node without the hot tier serves it again;
- a multipart upload begun on the hot tier (its upload id begins `ts-hot~`);
- an object whose marker was never written, which the reconciler's sweep of strays finds after `hot_store.stray_grace`.

So the reconciler must go on until the hot tier owes nothing, and only a process with the hot tier runs it. Turn the processes off one at a time and keep the last one on the hot tier until the checks below pass. While processes with and without the hot tier share the cold bucket, an object written through one with it is seen by one without it only once it is copied, and a delete through one without it does not stop a copy that was still owed, so keep that time short.

Before the last process is turned off:

1. `tierstore format-epoch --check --hot <tier>` lists every registration: `live` for a process still on the hot tier, `silent` for one turned off once its beats have lapsed. It then refuses unless each silent one is named with `--departed <id>`; the list is the part to read.
2. The marks bucket has no marker that says `DIRTY`, `LOCK` or `CLEARING`, and none with `gone` true. A `WANT` marker owes nothing.
3. Every object of the hot bucket outside `_tiering/`, `_journal/` and `_entries/` is in the cold bucket with the same size, and with the same tag where both tags are plain MD5 sums; compare the bytes of the others.
4. The hot bucket has no multipart upload in progress.

After that the hot bucket and the marks bucket are read by nothing; delete them when the time to go back has passed.

## Metrics

`GET /metricz` on the S3 listener (unsigned) answers 200 in the Prometheus text format. It has the hit, miss, byte and cold-read counters of the hot and cold tiers, the counters of the OCI read front, such as `tierstore_oci_requests_total`, and the counters of the cache of a build node with, on a node that serves jobscheduler's mount provider, the histogram of each job's time from its request to its start (`tierstore_request_to_start_seconds`, not in a release yet) and the time of each attach of a tree on a lookup of `tierstore fs` and where its manifests came from (`tierstore_fs_tree_attach_seconds`, `tierstore_fs_manifest_fetch_total` and `tierstore_fs_manifest_fill_seconds`, which the mount reports to the node) and the cache files each mount holds open and its opens that failed for lack of descriptors (`tierstore_fs_cache_descriptors` and `tierstore_fs_open_descriptor_failures_total`, which the mount reports too), all named `tierstore_` and a word; without an `oci` interface it says the front is not enabled, and a node with no cache says so on a line of its own. The full list of metric names, their labels and queries to alert on is in [metrics](metrics.md).

## Handing out read URLs

A job that holds no key reads its objects with presigned GET URLs. The service that starts the job signs one URL for each object with a key that may read (a scoped key limited to the job's prefix is the usual choice; see [keys](keys.md)) and gives the job the URLs. Each URL is good for at most `server.presign_max_expires` (15 minutes by default), so sign it when the job starts, and again for a job that runs longer. To fetch a large object in parallel, the job sends several `GET`s of the one URL, each with its own `Range` header, and puts the parts together: the range is not signed, so every part passes. A URL is a bearer credential; do not log it, and use TLS where the path is not trusted. A URL the server refuses is always a 403, and its code says why: `AccessDenied` (the URL has expired, its method is not GET or HEAD, it asks for more than one object or carries a parameter beyond the SigV4 ones, `x-id` and the six `response-*` ones, `host` is not a signed header, the key may not read the object, or the request has no signature at all); `InvalidAccessKeyId` (the key id is unknown, is a GC key, or the region is not the server's); `SignatureDoesNotMatch` (a wrong secret, or the URL was changed after it was signed, its method and path included); `AuthorizationQueryParametersError` (`X-Amz-Expires` is zero or over `server.presign_max_expires`); `RequestTimeTooSkewed` (`X-Amz-Date` is more than 15 minutes ahead of the server's clock); `AuthorizationHeaderMalformed` (a SigV4 parameter is missing, repeated or not well formed). The first thing to check is the clock of the signing service against the server's.
