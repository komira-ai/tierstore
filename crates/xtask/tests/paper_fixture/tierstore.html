tierstore: a tiered object store, from a node’s own NVMe to distributed hard disks

Past RAM, no memory controller moves data between fast and slow storage for you. tierstore is that layer for objects: a local cache on a node’s own NVMe, a hot store spread over NVMe and SSD, and a cold store spread over hard disks, behind one object API. Why we built it, how it behaves, and the case study of our build farm.

A CPU never asks a program where a value lives. L1, L2 and L3 caches and RAM sit behind one address, and the memory controller moves data between them as it is used. Past RAM that stops. A node’s own NVMe, a distributed store on flash and a distributed store on hard disks each have their own interface, failures and cost per byte, and every system that uses more than one writes its own tiering. tierstore is a tiering layer for that part of the hierarchy. It gives one object interface over three kinds of tier and decides where each object lives.

The problem. Hard disks are cheap per byte and slow for many small writes. Flash is fast and costs more per byte. A workload that writes very many small objects and rereads the same ones suits neither alone, and a tiering rule written inside one store serves that store only.

The hypotheses.

  1. A workload that rereads a fairly stable set of objects is judged by how rarely a read reaches the slowest tier, so the layer answers from the fastest tier that holds the object.
  2. A write is acknowledged once it is durable on fast media, and the move to disks happens later in large batches.
  3. If no object is ever overwritten, a retry, a restart or a repeated copy cannot change bytes that a reader already trusts.
  4. One proxy with a small contract for the stores behind it lets any store that meets the contract be a tier.

The solution. tierstore is a stateless S3 proxy with three kinds of tier. A local_cache keeps the inputs a node rereads on that node’s own NVMe. A hot_store, on NVMe or SSD across several nodes, takes new writes and holds recent objects. A cold_store, on hard disks across several nodes, keeps everything. A put is acknowledged when the object and a journal entry are durable on the hot store, and a reconciler holding a lease copies objects to the cold store in the background and frees the hot store when it fills. A get reads the fastest tier that holds the object and fills a faster tier on a miss. Writes are create-only.

This paper is about tierstore, its design and the evidence for it. Our build farm is the first application and the only workload we have measured, and no farm traffic goes through v1 yet, so sections 1 and 5 are a case study marked with a Case study label, and farm-specific detail elsewhere sits in side notes. Section 2 is the architecture, section 3 the interface, section 4 permissions and section 6 correctness. Section 7 lists what we have not measured.

Case study

1. Our build farm, the problem

Our build farm is the first workload we design tierstore for, and so far the only one. v1 runs in place on all six nodes, but no farm traffic goes through it yet. It hurts in both directions. A build writes very many small objects, which our hard disks handled badly, and it reads the same inputs again and again. This section covers the disks, the repeat reads and parallel fetch, and section 5 covers what we run and build for them.

Hard disks and many small writes

Our object store was MinIO on 12 hard disks, two in each of six servers, with 8 TB, 5400 rpm drives that report drive-managed shingled recording. They suit capacity and do not suit many small writes. In one reading all 12 were 87 to 100% busy writing 0.2 to 0.9 MB/s at an average write of 7 KB, and 71% of the writes since the pods started took longer than 10 seconds. One action output of 15,189 files (307 MB) needed 15,189 writes, 30,378 reads and 15,189 consensus commits, and its upload ran for 43 minutes and failed.

Case study

These figures are measurements of our farm’s disks and flash from before tierstore existed. The kbf paper has the detail.

Flash in front is the first remedy. A write tier acknowledges new objects on flash and writes them to the disks in large batches. On a node whose flash was a SATA SSD it took a cold unpack of the toolchain from 243 seconds to 33, and the flush drained 306 MB in about nine minutes at 0.6 MB/s, so the disks stayed the limit for flushing and stopped being the limit for building. That write tier lived inside one system. We wanted the same behaviour for any two stores that meet the contract in section 3.

The other two places for flash are an input cache on each compute node, which matters more to this workload and which section 5 adds, and packing many small objects into one large one. Packing is kbf’s design and uses tierstore.

Edge cases: a second cause, and who does the packing

The disks were not the only cause. Later stalls and mid-transfer connection drops came mostly from the pod network, which ran VXLAN inside a VPN overlay (the kbf paper has the retransmit counts). We count that as a second, separate cause.

kbf’s write tier is the first of the three. tierstore is the same idea as a separate S3 service: it is installed beside the write tier. Section 5 adds the second, as a tier of a chained tierstore. Packing is done by kbf’s pack code in whatever writes the pack: the node daemon when it is started with its pack-write flag (off by default), a frontend, and in the planned shape the task. The frontend indexes and verifies packs, and tierstore stores a pack as one object. tierstore’s part is the capability underneath: many requests in flight at once, ranged reads into a large object, and multipart uploads, so a client such as kbf can fetch a pack’s parts in parallel.

These measurements are about writes. The next two subsections are about reads. They are the reason the second place, the input cache on each compute node, matters more to this workload than the first.

In the build farm most reads are repeats. The same compiler and library files are inputs to thousands of actions on each node, and the set of distinct inputs changes slowly. A tiered store for that workload is judged by how rarely a read reaches the cold store. By arithmetic, a cache that holds everything it has seen and never evicts would serve about 99% of the input bytes of this build from local storage (1 minus distinct over total), and about 98% of the bytes in blobs of 64 MiB or less. Read this with care.

  • No cross-check. The frontends keep no durable per-run history, so there was no per-run input-byte figure to compare the closures with (validation count 0: nothing was checked). The closure walk is the only source. A hand check of two small input roots is still owed.
  • A ceiling, not a forecast. A node’s first read of a blob is a miss by definition and is counted as distinct, but the ratio assumes a cache that never evicts and never fails. It is not a hit rate.
  • Bytes are not time. This bounds the transfer a cache removes, not the wall-clock it saves. Whether input fetch is on the critical path of an action needs a per-action fetch time that we do not record.
  • One build, one window. The build had no retries; each action ran once. A day of builds would add repeats between builds, which would raise the ratio, and that is not measured.
  • Placement is the scheduler’s of that day. Placing actions by load could spread the readers of a closure differently. With ratios above 40 we do not expect the verdict to be sensitive to that, which is a judgment and not a measurement.
  • Blob granularity. The count is per blob. A cache stores packs and objects, so the ratio that applies to it is the one per pack. We have not run that count, because no map from blob to pack was available.
  • Large blobs. The two thirds of the bytes in blobs over 64 MiB do not reach the pack store today; they stay on the storage nodes. A cache serves them only once large objects can be packed or cached, so the first claim for a pack cache is the share without them, where each distinct byte is read 47 to 64 times.

Parallel fetch: many small requests

Through tierstore on one node, reading an 18,105-file toolchain tree took 176.4 seconds with 1 request in flight and 12.6 seconds with 64. Section 2 has the runs and their limits. Parallel fetch is tierstore’s capability, because it serves many requests at once. Packing small files into a few large objects is a separate lever and is kbf’s design (the kbf paper).

Reading the 176.4 to 12.6 seconds. At one request in flight most of the 176.4 seconds is the cost per request, about 9 ms, so the gain comes from running requests in parallel.

What the farm needed

Three needs follow. Many small writes must land on flash, away from the hard disks. Repeat reads must be served next to the work that repeats them. A client that fetches many small objects must be able to keep many requests in flight. Section 2 gives the architecture that answers them and section 5 shows how it runs on our farm.

2. The architecture: local_cache, hot_store and cold_store

Why three tiers

A CPU cache hierarchy works because a program rereads a small set of addresses, so the slow level is reached rarely. A tiered object store is judged the same way, by how seldom a read reaches its slowest tier. Each tier answers one fact about the workload.

  • The local_cache sits on a node’s own NVMe and serves repeat reads next to the work.
  • The hot_store is spread over NVMe or SSD on several nodes. It takes new writes and acknowledges them on fast media, so the hard disks never see the many small writes, and it holds recent objects.
  • The cold_store is spread over hard disks on several nodes. It keeps everything at the lowest cost per byte and receives its writes in large batches.

Two rules make the tiers fit. Every write is create-only, so a retry, a restart or a repeated copy cannot change bytes that a reader already trusts. Each tier needs only the small contract of section 3, so any store that meets it can be any tier.

What the design is judged by

Four behaviours that work against it

The design is judged by how rarely a read reaches the cold store. A fast acknowledgement and a hot store that holds the working set matter because they keep reads away from the slow tier. Four behaviours of the code as it stands work against that, and we name each below: two cold reads in every put, eviction that ignores reads, no single-flight on a cold miss, and a ranged miss that reads and copies the whole object. Section 5 turns them into requirements, and the design requires each.

The data path

Put

When the hot store has room, a put runs these steps in order and is acknowledged only after the last.

Put, step by step
  1. Ask cold. A HEAD of the key on the cold store. If the key exists the put is refused with 412, and if the cold store cannot answer the put fails.
  2. Create in hot. A create-only write to the hot store. If the key exists there, 412.
  3. Ask cold again. The first ask can be stale, because the reconciler copies an object to cold before it deletes it from hot. If cold now holds the key, the proxy removes the object it created and answers 412 (figure in section 6). If cold cannot answer, it removes the object and fails the put.
  4. Journal. A journal object, _journal/<proxy>/<seq>, holding the entry, batched with a 20 ms wait for more entries and written create-only to hot. If that write fails, the proxy removes the hot object and fails the put.
  5. Acknowledge.

A put therefore costs two HEADs of the cold store, an erasure-coded write to hot where the hot store codes that way, and a journal write. Its latency and availability are tied to the slow store that the design exists to hide, so a put of an object that is read many times later pays two trips to the slow tier before it is acknowledged. For a key chosen by content a collision can only be the same bytes, so the two checks could be skipped or made lazy. Today they are neither, and section 5 lists the change as a requirement. We have not measured either cost.

Edge cases: a full hot store, and what we have not measured for puts

We have not measured a slow cold store slowing every put, a cold store that cannot answer failing them, or puts with the hot set degraded, which every rolling update of a distributed hot store does to it.

When the hot store is full. At the cap, or when the hot store answers that it is full, a put that is not a presigned write goes straight to the cold store: create-only, not journaled, and acknowledged when the cold store has it. It is as durable as the cold store and as slow as it is. A presigned write never takes that path and answers 503 instead, so the writer retries. A multipart completion is cold-only in the same way. A streamed put that the hot store rejects after taking part of the body fails with a full error, and the client’s retry goes to the cold store. Above 95% of the cap a put first waits 20 ms.

Get

Get and eviction, in detail

Hot first, then cold. A hot answer other than a not-found is returned as it is, so a read that the hot store cannot serve fails even when the cold store holds the object. A cold hit is read whole, even for a ranged request, and copied back into hot before the read returns, unless hot is past its critical mark (95% of the cap by default). Three costs work against a repeat-read workload.

  • Eviction ignores reads. It takes the oldest object by the time in its journal entry, which is write or copy-back order, so it is first in, first out and not least recently used. An object that every job reads can be evicted, read from cold, copied back and queued again. Read-aware eviction is a requirement in section 5.
  • There is no single-flight. N concurrent misses of one key do N cold reads and N hot writes, and all but one of the writes find the key present. Single-flight is a requirement in section 5.
  • A ranged miss copies the whole object. A 4 KiB range of a 128 MiB pack reads and copies 32,768 times what was asked, and a sweep over cold objects churns the evict-and-fill cycle.
Edge cases: HEAD, listing and buffering

A cold hit’s latency includes a write to hot. HEAD is the same lookup without the copy-back, and a listing merges both stores with hot winning. A GET returns its body as one buffer in the proxy’s memory. A streaming GET does not exist today (section 7).

Delete, and what is consistent

Delete and consistency, in detail

A delete removes the key from hot, then from cold, then appends a delete entry to the journal. It is not atomic across the two stores, so the reconciler can have read the object from hot before the delete and put it into cold after it. That copy is removed only when the reconciler applies the delete entry, and until then a deleted key can still be listed and, by code reading, read from cold.

Read-after-write holds for a new key. List-after-delete does not hold until the reconciler applies the delete entry, so a client that lists a prefix to confirm a delete can see deleted keys. The window is the copy lag, which we have not measured.

Reconciler

The reconciler, in detail

One reconciler runs at a time, guarded by a lease object in the hot store. It reads each proxy’s journal from a checkpoint, copies the listed objects to cold with create-only writes, and when hot passes 0.8 of its cap evicts the oldest objects down to 0.7, only those behind the checkpoint that cold already holds. It prunes applied journals after 24 hours by default and publishes usage. It never lists the data prefixes, so neither store is scanned. The table gives the binary’s defaults.

3. One interface over all of them

A caller of tierstore sees one object store. It does not choose a tier, keep a cache coherent or prune one. Behind that interface tierstore fills a faster tier on a miss, copies new objects to the cold_store, evicts from the hot_store when it fills and prunes its own journal. How stale a listing or a cached copy of a deleted key can be is in section 6.

The interface follows the configuration

The protocol tierstore speaks to a store is set by that store’s configuration, through its kind. Configure an S3 store and tierstore speaks S3 to it. The front that callers see is S3 for every kind, so any S3 library, command-line tool or person can use it as they would any S3 endpoint. Support for Google Cloud Storage and other object stores is added through the same configuration, as new kinds of store.

The kinds in the file are s3, dir and memory. The local_cache is designed as a further table. tierstore is a proxy and keeps no durable state of its own. Its journal, checkpoint and lease objects live in the hot store, and it has no metadata database and no per-object flag. We built a proxy because a client sees one endpoint, the stores underneath need only a small contract, and the tiering rule is ours and testable where a store’s own transition feature would be that store’s rule. The limits of the interface are under Known limits in section 6.

What the caller does not have to do

  • Pick a tier. There is one endpoint and one name space, and a read is answered from the fastest tier that holds the object.
  • Keep a cache coherent. A local_cache holds only create-only keys that name their content, so nothing needs invalidating. A deleted key can stay readable from a cache (section 6).
  • Prune anything. Hot eviction, journal retention and the size of the local_cache are tierstore’s work.
  • Know how many tiers there are. A stock SDK needs a few settings, listed under Known limits in section 6.

The contract a store must meet

The contract in full

The contract is narrower than full S3 compatibility. A store must be reachable over plain HTTP, because the backend’s client refuses https (“TLS is not built yet”). It must honour If-None-Match: * on a put, and on a multipart completion if it is the cold store, because every guarantee rests on create-only. The hot store must honour If-Match on a put, which the lease and the checkpoints use, although today’s start-up test asks both stores (section 6). Every store must answer a ranged read with 206, a HEAD with Last-Modified, and a listing that pages by start-after. A plain-HTTP, self-hosted store that does all that can be a tier, and so can another tierstore. A store that serves only https, which includes the managed services, cannot be one until TLS is built, and neither can a store that ignores conditional headers.

Topologies

The same binary runs in every position. What a tierstore does follows only from the tiers its configuration names, and there is no role setting. Three arrangements matter to us.

One node with all tiers. A single tierstore names a local_cache, a hot_store and a cold_store, all on one machine. It is the simplest way to run the whole design.

Storage nodes with a hot_store and a cold_store. Several tierstores run over one hot_store on NVMe or SSD and one cold_store on hard disks. A tierstore keeps no state that a peer needs, so any one of them serves any key. The journal, the lease and the reconciler exist only where a hot_store is configured, so they run on these nodes and nowhere else.

Example: build nodes chaining a local_cache in front of the storage tierstore. A tierstore on a build node names a local_cache on the node’s own disk and a cold_store that is the storage nodes’ tierstore, reached over S3. The build node has no hot_store, so no journal, lease or background copy runs there. This is the shape of our next-generation build farm (section 5).

Chaining tierstores

A tierstore is itself an S3 store that meets the tier contract, so one tierstore can be another’s tier. The interface a caller sees does not change with the depth, and one binary and one interface build a hierarchy of more than two levels.

Chaining: the rules and the edge cases
  • No role. What a tierstore does follows only from the tiers its configuration names.
  • Three kinds of tier. A tier is a hot_store, a cold_store or a local_cache, backed by memory, a local directory or an S3 endpoint. All three backings exist today (--hot and --cold take memory, dir:<path> or s3:<bucket> with an endpoint), and local_cache is the new kind (section 2).
  • Chaining depth. The one case the design covers is the build node’s cold_store (section 5). We claim no deeper chain, and a tierstore whose cold_store names its own address should be refused at start-up, which is our requirement.

We checked the contract clause by clause against the crate as the code reads. By that reading the front meets it for a cold_store, and nobody has yet run one tierstore over another.

A second thing makes several storage endpoints plausible. A storage tierstore keeps no state a peer needs. Each journal, checkpoint and lease object lives in the hot store, the binary’s documentation says any number of these processes may run over the same two stores, and the six proxies that ran on the farm before v1 went live did, over one hot and one cold store. A put through one is in the hot store (or, when hot is full, the cold store) when it is acknowledged, and a read through another asks the hot store first, so read-after-write holds across them for a new key. Detail of each piece the design adds for chaining
  • The requirements of section 5. Read-aware eviction on the hot tier, single-flight at every tier, no cold existence check for digest-named keys, ranged fills that stay small, and counters for hits and cold reads.
  • The local_cache kind, with its write-through, its read fill and its LRU on disk within a byte budget. The crate has a read cache in memory with LRU; it is a precedent for the eviction rule and not for a cache on disk.
  • A list of cold endpoints and the pick. --cold-endpoint takes one address. The list, the rendezvous hash, the failover rule and what a retried put does are all new. A put that failed over after a 5xx may have been applied; the retry then gets 412, which for a digest-named key means the same bytes, by the argument and the condition in section 6, and the client has to have a rule for it.
  • Optional tiers. Today --hot and --hot-bytes are required, and every process starts a proxy, a front and a reconciler that works whenever it holds the lease. A process with no hot_store, and a journal, lease and reconciler that start only with one, are a change to how the binary is assembled.
  • A start-up test for a tier that is a tierstore. As written, a tierstore started over another tierstore would be refused at its first write (403) and counted as a violation, by our reading of the code. The test must stop asking a tier that is not a hot_store the If-Match question, and its probe keys must be somewhere the front allows. Nobody has run a tierstore over a tierstore: the tests of the front drive it with kbf’s own S3 client, the one the S3 backend is built on, against in-process memory tiers, and the tests of the S3 backend run against a fake store.
  • A streaming GET. A GET today is answered as one buffer in the proxy’s memory, and the design does not address it. Through a chain it matters for packs: the first byte of a miss on a 128 MiB object arrives only after the whole read, and the memory of a storage tierstore under several concurrent fills is that many objects. Either a GET streams, or fills are by range and small; which is open.

Why a proxy, and not something that exists

A block cache under the disks, a file system with a flash metadata device, MinIO’s lifecycle transition, Ceph’s cache tiering and JuiceFS or Alluxio each tier at some other layer. We chose a proxy because one S3 endpoint serves every client, the rule is ours and testable, and the stores underneath stay unmodified. tierstore is its own crate in the kbf repository, and it is the one binary that both storage nodes and build nodes run in the case study of section 5.

The alternatives we know from documentation

We know the alternatives from their documentation and list them so that a reader can judge. A block cache under the disks (bcache, dm-writecache, lvmcache) would absorb small writes below the store, per disk. It is the nearest alternative and we did not try it. A file system with a small-block or metadata device on flash, such as ZFS with a special device and a separate log device, is the same idea one layer up. MinIO’s lifecycle transition moves objects to a remote tier by age: it does not absorb writes, and the tiering rule would be the store’s own. Ceph’s cache tiering was the closest design inside a distributed store, and Ceph has deprecated it. Layers such as JuiceFS or Alluxio put a cache between client and store. We chose a proxy because one S3 endpoint serves every client, the rule is ours and testable, and the stores underneath stay unmodified.

Permissions answer two questions. How does tierstore decide what a caller may do, and which credentials does tierstore hold toward the stores behind it? The second answer is why a job never holds the credential of a tier.

Edge cases of presigned writes

A 412 on a retry is ambiguous. If the first PUT committed and its answer was lost, the retry gets 412 and cannot tell its own earlier success from a PUT by someone else who held the URL. The signature does not cover the body, so the uploader must read the object back and compare its size and digest. AWS S3 itself can answer 409 for overlapping conditional writes to one key where tierstore answers 412, so a client’s retry logic should accept both.

Who may delete under a presigned-write prefix. The GC key is the only key meant to delete there. The full key can delete anywhere, and we keep it away from untrusted writers by rule, with no code enforcing that.

The proxy verifies a presigned write itself and does not trust it to the store, because stores differ in how they treat the signed parts of a URL. A deploy can probe each store for those differences before a proxy starts.

A scoped key, which limits the read to prefixes and has no delete prefix, is the later option if a bucket-wide read is not acceptable. A job on the same node must not be able to reach that key, whichever kind it is.

The fences of section 6 were argued for a job that presents a presigned URL to the proxy that holds the stores. A chain changes where each is checked. Where a job’s write ends is taken by the design to be the tierstore on the job’s own node, which checks the URL and writes through to the cold_store, at the price of moving the trust boundary to the build node, so we say what each place costs.

  • A presigned URL names one host. A SigV4 presigned URL signs its host header, and the proxy refuses a URL that does not. A URL minted for one storage endpoint is invalid at another, so presigned writes do not combine with a per-request pick across endpoints, unless the endpoints share one authority name. If the URL ends at the storage tierstore, the write skips the build node’s local_cache and its write-through. If it ends at the build node’s tierstore, that tierstore must verify the URL and forward the write with a key of its own; the prefix fence, the size cap and the expiry are then checked at the build node, and the storage tier sees only the build node’s key. The trust boundary moves to the build node, which is the key problem above.
  • One winner holds on one path. Two presigned PUTs of one key have one winner because a presigned put never takes the hot-full path to cold. A write forwarded by a build node is presigned at the storage tier, by the build node’s tierstore with its presigning key, so it should take the same path and have one winner; a test must show that, including when hot is full, because a write that is not presigned goes to cold when hot is full and can race a put into hot (section 6, known limits). Through a chain, the guarantee of section 6 should hold on the forwarded path too, and until that test passes it is shown only for a presigned put sent directly.
  • Job writes. A job will write to an output path fenced to it, with no delete. The pieces: in tierstore, the presigned PUT and the GC key (above); in kbf’s frontend, the commit of an untrusted pack, the ingest of a task’s pack with its key mint, read grants, and the step that gives a run its key, its presigned write and its read grant after checking that its inputs are held. The executor that calls that step is the next design step.

5. Our build farm, the solution

The solution comes in two versions. v1 is the first layout, and it is what runs: all six Linux nodes run tierstore with a local_cache on the node’s SSD, written through to a cold store on hard disks, with no hot store. v2 is the shape we design for, with chained tierstores and a hot_store on dedicated flash, and it is not measured. A sentence in the present tense describes the tierstore code, and a sentence with will describes what the design adds, each with a number to measure when it runs.

v2: chained tierstores and dedicated storage nodes

Storage nodes run a hot_store on flash and a cold_store on hard disks, and with them the journal, the lease and the reconciler. Each compute node runs a local_cache on its own NVMe drive in front of the storage nodes’ tierstore, which is its cold_store, reached over S3. A put on a compute node is written through and acknowledged when the storage tier acknowledges. A read looks at the local drive first and fills it on a miss. How many storage nodes there will be is open. It is set by the read load the local caches leave for the storage tier, the capacity the hot_store needs and the node losses its layout has to survive.

Details of v2: storage nodes, build nodes, endpoints, upgrades and no network
  • Storage nodes. A storage node will run tierstore with a hot_store on its U.2 flash and a cold_store on its 15 hard disks. The journal, the lease and the reconciler, the background process that copies hot to cold and evicts, will exist only when a hot_store is configured, so they will run only on storage nodes.
  • Build nodes. A build (compute) node will run tierstore with a local_cache on the node’s own NVMe drive and a cold_store that is the storage nodes’ tierstore, spoken to over S3. A build node has no hot tier, so no journal, lease or background copy runs there, by construction and with no setting for it.
  • Several storage endpoints (a proposal). The cold_store of a build node may list several storage endpoints; three storage nodes is the example, and how many there will be is open; the list follows whatever number is chosen. A storage tierstore keeps no state that a peer needs, so any one of them serves any key and read-after-write holds across them. Each one still has its own --proxy-id, which must be unique among the processes over the same tiers and kept across restarts, and its journal lives in the hot store under that id. The proposal is a pick per request by a rendezvous hash of the key, with failover to the next endpoint on a connect error, a 5xx, or a 503 from /readyz, which means the endpoint is draining. Several endpoints give redundancy of the process and not of the stores: they share one hot store and one cold store, so a fault in either answers 5xx at every endpoint.
  • Upgrades. Storage tierstores will roll one at a time: on SIGTERM a tierstore already answers 503 on /readyz, serves on for 10 seconds by default so that a caller sees it, refuses new requests, waits up to 900 seconds by default for those in flight, gives the lease up and exits. The lease moves, and the reconciler resumes from its checkpoint (section 6). What a replacement does to the journal of the id it replaces is not traced (open questions below). Build-node tierstores may restart at any time: the cache holds digest-named keys, so its contents can be kept or dropped.
  • Build commands with no network. A build command will run with no network, and a job will read its inputs from its own node. How the bytes get from the node’s tierstore to a command that has no socket is decided for the first version: the task process, which does have a network, reads the inputs from the node’s tierstore before the command starts and writes them where the command can read them. Mounting whole large objects read-only instead of copying them is an option, decided after the transport between job and cache and the copy cost have been measured (open questions below). The sandbox that enforces it belongs to kbf and is described in the kbf paper. The job scheduler’s job spec has no network setting, and its action container already gets a new network namespace with loopback only; we have not traced which path the farm’s kbf jobs take.

Why this shape

Measurement 1 sets what is at stake. Each node read its distinct input bytes 111 to 133 times in one cold build, so if a cache holds the repeats, the bytes a node fetches from outside are a small part of the bytes its actions read. That is a ceiling for one build and not a forecast. The shape follows from it, with a cache next to the reader and the rest of the hierarchy out of the way of repeat reads. The cache holds only keys that name their content (section 6), so the same interface serves a job, a tool and a person, and only the distance the bytes travel changes.

The bar to beat: today’s paths for a job’s inputs, and the 9.02 ms cost

The read run in section 2 shows a median per-request cost of 9.02 ms for a file of about 19 KB, with client and proxy on one host. A local_cache on the build node’s own disk removes the storage tiers’ share of that cost for a hit, such as the read across nodes of the Silo hot tier of that run. If a task’s fetch speaks signed HTTP S3 to its node’s tierstore, which is decided for the first version, a hit pays most of the proxy and client cost per file, and may recover only that share. That is untested. The baselines in section 2 give the number to beat.

The bar is high. Today a job’s inputs do not come from tierstore. The farm’s node daemon keeps inputs on local flash and hard-links them into each action, so no bytes are copied. A cache reached over a network interface copies bytes and must be measured against that. kbf also has a job-facing read listener on its frontend. A task on a worker fetches one blob per HTTPS request with a read grant for its Action, and the listener refuses a blob over 64 MiB. It is off unless configured, the step that issues the grants is not called yet, and we have not checked whether the listener runs on the farm.

Beyond the build farm, as far as the design allows

Nothing in the hierarchy names a build. Other workloads that reread a stable set of objects, such as layers of container images, mirrors of packages, or model weights and datasets that many workers read, have the same shape on paper. We have run none of them and hold no number for any, so this is what the design allows. A workload that reads each object once from a cold set much larger than the hot tier would have a low hit rate by nature, and the admission rule of section 6 is meant to keep such a scan from displacing a working set.

What we did not choose

We have tried none of these; they are what we know from documentation, listed so that a reader can judge.

  • For v2, a local_cache over a plain S3 store, with no hot tier at all. That is the shape of v1. Section 3 argues that if each job writes one pack, the disks see sequential writes and the write tier matters less. In v2 we keep a hot_store on the storage nodes all the same, and with it the journal, lease and reconciler, which are the source of most of the limits in section 6. We have not settled why it stays. The bar it has to meet is the test of the v1 section: a different, faster device, or shared across nodes. Measurement 1 does not bear on it: it counts reads. Candidates it has not weighed are writers of small objects, such as the Macs and other clients, and the latency of a put on hard disks. The reason is open, and the v1 shape decides it: it runs with no hot tier, so the acknowledgement time of small puts on the hard disks, and the load of small writers such as the Macs, show what a hot tier buys (open questions below).
  • The daemon’s hard-link cache. It exists, copies no bytes and is the number to beat. It is a cache inside kbf and not an S3 endpoint, so a tool or a person cannot use it, and it is not the path a job on a worker takes.
  • A caching reverse proxy in front of the storage tierstore. Immutable keys suit one. But every request is SigV4-signed over its host, headers and query, so a general cache either keys each request differently or ignores the signature and checks nothing; and it has no write-through with create-only semantics. A tierstore with a local_cache is the part that knows those rules.
  • Peer fill between build nodes. It would take load off the storage tier. It needs build nodes to serve each other and a way to find a holder; it is not part of the shape we design for and we have not explored it.

6. Correctness and failure handling

This section says why the design is safe and what happens when something fails. Fence 1, who may write, is the table of key kinds in section 4. Everything here comes from tests, code reading and one sequential probe of each store, and none of it from a run of the proxy under load.

Create-only writes

A wrong object that is read costs a rebuild or a retry. An object that is overwritten corrupts every reader that trusts it. The design is one invariant and four fences.

The invariant is that nothing is overwritten. Every put is create-only, and a put without If-None-Match is treated the same way. If-Match on an object PUT is refused with 412 when the tag differs from the stored object’s and with 501 when it matches, because an object cannot be replaced. Any If-None-Match other than * is refused with 501.

Create-only refuses a put while the key exists, and a delete frees the name. What makes a retry, a restart or a replayed reconciler pass safe is that the repeat writes the same bytes. That holds by construction for a key chosen by content and is an assumption for any other key.

Fence 1: who may write

The key kinds in section 4 are the first fence. An untrusted writer holds only a presigning key.

Fence 2: partial objects

A put is streamed to the stores through a bounded queue of four pieces, and the object becomes visible only after the last byte arrives and every checksum matches. A failure leaves no object, no temporary file and no journal entry. A declared length over the limit (256 MiB by default) is refused with 413 before any byte is read, at most 32 puts run at once, and a slow client is cut after a grace period.

Edge cases of fence 2: sizes, lengths and slow clients

A put is streamed to the stores through a bounded queue of four pieces, and the object becomes visible only after the last byte arrives and every checksum matches. On an S3 store it is one PUT up to 16 MiB, or a multipart upload in 16 MiB parts completed create-only. A failure leaves no object, no temporary file and no journal entry. A request with no length where the store needs one answers 411. A request that declares a length over the limit (256 MiB by default, or the presigning key’s ceiling if that is smaller) is refused with 413 before any byte is read, and a body that turns out longer than it declared is counted on the bytes received and refused with nothing stored. At most 32 puts run at once (a further one answers 503 with Retry-After: 1). A client that sends under 1,024 bytes a second, counted only over the time the proxy spent waiting for it, is cut after 30 seconds of grace. The rule reads a clock that cannot go backward.

Fence 3: races between the stores

A key can move between the two stores while a put is in flight. The proxy asks cold, creates the object in hot, and asks cold again. If the key was copied to cold and evicted from hot in between, the second ask finds it, and the proxy removes what it created and answers 412. This is sound because the reconciler copies before it deletes. Two presigned PUTs of one key have one winner, provided the hot store’s If-None-Match: * is atomic under concurrent writers.

Fence 3 in detail

The recheck in step 3 is the second of the two cold HEADs that section 2 says work against a repeat-read workload. It closes a real race for keys of any kind, and for a key chosen by content it can be skipped or made lazy, which section 5 lists as a requirement. Until that is built the fence costs a cold read on every put.

Edge cases of fence 3: why the one-winner claim is not yet measured

Two presigned PUTs of one key have one winner, whether they reach one proxy or two, provided the hot store’s If-None-Match: * is atomic under concurrent writers. A presigned put never takes the hot-full path to cold, so the hot store’s create-only write alone decides. The start-up test and the probe ask one request at a time and cannot show atomicity. The history of MinIO has a case where it failed (below). The tests that back the claim run against directory, memory and loopback stores and a stand-in cold store, and no concurrent race on Silo is recorded. The box below measures it.

Fence 4: the backend must tell the truth

Create-only is worth nothing if the store underneath ignores it, or honours it only until it loses quorum. A start-up test checks the first, and nothing in tierstore can check the second.

Before it binds its address, tierstore asks each store a set of questions, one request at a time. A wrong answer exits with status 3 and is not restarted, and an unreachable store is retried for 5 minutes before exit with status 1. The test is a smoke test. It cannot show that a store honours conditional headers atomically under concurrent writers, or with part of its redundancy down.

Edge cases of the start-up test

Before it binds its address, tierstore asks each store a set of questions, one request at a time: create-only put, head, If-Match, an exact range, paging by start-after, and, for the cold store, a create-only multipart completion. A wrong answer exits with status 3 and is not restarted. An unreachable store is retried for 5 minutes, with a pause that starts at 1 second and doubles to 30, and then exits with status 1. A full store (507) skips the write checks. Only the multipart check may be skipped, and the log says why. Before this test the S3 backend assumed that the store honoured conditional puts.

This is a smoke test for a store that ignores conditional headers. It cannot show that a store honours them atomically under concurrent writers, or with part of its redundancy down. It does not probe continuation tokens or max-keys (the client follows tokens itself), Last-Modified values, the refusal of a bad part list, or the presigned path. A deploy can probe the presigned path once against each store directly (section 4).

A distributed store can accept a conditional put when it cannot reach its read quorum, and then an existing object can be overwritten. MinIO had exactly that bug (MinIO issue 21603, fixed upstream in pull request 21653). It is why the guarantees depend on the store and not only on the proxy, and why a node down, as in any rolling update of a distributed store, is a normal state.

Crashes, and the lease

Crash recovery, in detail

The reconciler resumes from its checkpoint after a crash and redoes work, which is harmless only when the redone step finds the same bytes. Copies are create-only, and the delete of a cold copy is skipped if hot holds the key again with the same content. Eviction from hot is not conditioned. A copy that finds the key already in cold is counted as copied without comparing size or content tag, which is safe for a content-addressed key and an assumption for any other. The lease is taken create-only, renewed with If-Match and, after its time to live (30 seconds by default), taken over with If-Match, so of two takers one fails. No fencing token protects the eviction deletes that a paused holder may still issue.

Edge cases: the unchecked copy, journal faults, lease clocks and overlapping passes

A copy that finds the key already in cold is counted as copied without comparing size or content tag, and hot’s copy then becomes eligible for eviction. If cold holds a different object under that key, such as a leftover, a put that went straight to cold while hot was full, or another writer’s, the reconciler treats hot’s copy as safe, deletes it, and the cold bytes silently replace it. For a content-addressed key the two are the same bytes. For any other key that is an assumption. A fix would be to HEAD the cold object and compare size and tag before counting the key as copied. It is not made. In our reading, this fix adds a cold read to the copy, which is off the put path, once per object.

A corrupt journal object stops that proxy’s journal and is reported each pass. A gap in the journal numbers stops reading until it is filled. The lease is taken create-only and renewed with If-Match. After its time to live (30 seconds by default) it is taken over with If-Match, so of two takers one fails. Expiry compares the lease object’s last-modified time, as the hot store reports it, with the contender’s own clock. It therefore needs bounded skew between the store’s clock and each node’s, at a time to live of 30 seconds. No fencing token protects the eviction deletes that a paused holder may still issue. A holder learns it lost the lease only when it renews, once per pass. A pass longer than the lease can therefore overlap another holder and may store an older checkpoint over the new holder’s. The documented effect is an older checkpoint stored over the new holder’s. We have not analysed what follows from that. We do not claim that no data can be lost.

When the reconciler is dead, or blocked

A dead or blocked reconciler, in detail

A stale or missing usage counts as a hot store that is not full, so a dead reconciler gives no back-pressure and puts keep succeeding while hot fills. A live reconciler can also be stuck. It stops a proxy’s stream at the first object it cannot read or copy, so a permanent cold error on one key would block copy and eviction behind it for that proxy. An endpoint that answers 503 with a reason once usage is old exists today, and the copy lag grows in the published usage.

A put that went straight to cold is not journaled, so a delete entry of the same key that is applied after it deletes it. For content-addressed keys these are harmless. For other keys the copy-back against a delete and the unconditioned eviction still apply, and a re-put after a delete can write other bytes under a name that was freed.
  • A copy that finds the key in cold is accepted unchecked (above).
  • Head-of-line blocking and lease clocks (above).
  • Old journals after a restart. Journal objects before the stored checkpoint that are still younger than the retention are not read again, and are never deleted.
  • The directory backend syncs its directory. This applies to local-directory tiers, not to S3 stores.
  • The S3 backend needs a store that honours If-None-Match: * on a put and on a multipart completion. The start-up test checks that, one request at a time.
  • The S3 front serves a fixed list of calls. Copy, delimiter and version ids answer 501.
  • Cold is reached more than a repeat-read workload should need. Two HEADs per put, FIFO eviction that ignores reads, no single-flight on a cold miss and a whole-object copy for a ranged miss (section 2). Section 5 states the requirements that remove each, and the design requires them.

On the farm the non-presigned races matter less than they sound. A cas/b/ key carries a lease and a MAC and is written once, so a re-put after a delete is unlikely; the copy-back against a delete and the unconditioned eviction still apply to it. The S3-only and plain-HTTP limits are no obstacle on our own network, and no store that needs TLS has been tried.

The local_cache: what it needs to be safe

The local_cache design is create-only, content-addressed keys, write-through and LRU. A cache has no channel that tells it a key changed, so it holds only keys for which that cannot matter. A key that is the digest of its bytes cannot change meaning, so a cached copy is never wrong, if and only if the digest was verified before the name was accepted. A cached object can still be read after it was deleted from the storage tier, with no bound. A deletion that must take effect would need a purge by key or a maximum age, and the design has neither, which is a known limit.

The cache rules, in detail

Without the check, a bad first write is permanent. One write of the wrong bytes under a digest-shaped name makes every later writer of the real object get 412, until the full key deletes the name. We therefore require that the digest be checked on the write path before the name is accepted, and that the consumer verify each blob it reads, dropping a mismatching entry and fetching it once more and reporting a second mismatch as corrupt at the origin.

The cache never answers a create-only put, a 412 or an existence check from its own state, and it publishes a local copy only after the cold_store acknowledges. It refuses to cache keys that are not digest-named, the reserved prefixes _journal/ and _tiering/, a not-found, and listings. It inherits three requirements from section 2, which are single-flight fills, range or chunk fills, and an admission rule with read-aware eviction. Its purpose is to keep repeat reads off the tiers behind it (section 5), and a cache that is safe and often misses does not meet it.

Edge cases: stale reads, a bad first write, write-through rules, what the cache refuses and what it inherits

The local_cache is the tier in design. Its design is short: create-only, content-addressed keys, write-through, LRU. This section says what that needs in order to be safe, for a local_cache in front of any slower store; the build node is the case we have in mind. Where a rule is ours and goes beyond that core, it says so. Its purpose is to keep repeat reads off the tiers behind it (section 5): a cache that is safe and often misses does not meet it.

What the cache may hold

A cache has no channel that tells it a key changed. The local_cache therefore will hold only keys for which that cannot matter, and the design holds only create-only, digest-named keys.

When a stale cache is impossible for those keys. Create-only alone does not give it: a put is refused while the key exists, but a delete frees the name, and the same name can then be put with other bytes. A key that is the digest of its bytes cannot change meaning. A delete followed by a put of the same name can only carry the same bytes, so a cached copy is never wrong, if, and only if, the digest was verified before the name was accepted. It can be extra: a cached object can still be read after the object was deleted from the storage tier, and the reader gets the bytes the name stands for. Plainly, that is a read of a deleted key that succeeds, and it has no bound: LRU can keep an entry for as long as a stable workload keeps using it. A deletion that must take effect, such as a secret in a pack, would need a purge by key or a maximum age, and the design has neither. That is a limit and not an open question.

Without the check, a bad first write is permanent. Nothing in tierstore ties a key to its bytes today; a presigned URL does not even sign the body (section 4). With create-only and first writer wins, one write of the wrong bytes under a digest-shaped name makes every later writer of the real object get 412, and every cache and reader sees the wrong bytes until the full key deletes the name. So we require, as a rule beyond the core design, that the digest be checked on the write path before the name is accepted: the write-through path hashes the body in flight, and refuses, or never caches, a body whose digest differs from its name; or names are taken only from a writer that hashed, such as the frontend that hashes a pack before it commits it. The S3 backend already makes an object visible only after the whole body was read and checked, so a refusal before the object exists is possible in principle; we have not designed it. A check only on fill keeps wrong bytes from spreading to caches and leaves them at the origin. Who checks is open; checking on the write path is the only choice that stops the poisoning.

The consumer should check too. A digest name lets the reader verify end to end, which covers every hop: the cache, the storage tier and a bad first write. A cached entry is served without going back to the store, so an entry that fails the check needs a repair path or a retry reads the same entry. The requirement: a job’s fetch verifies each blob’s digest; on a mismatch the cache drops the entry and refetches once, and counts and logs it; a second mismatch is reported as corrupt at the origin. The task’s fetch already hashes each blob as it streams it to disk (against the frontend’s listener); that it would do the same against a tierstore is open.

Write-through rules (ours, to keep the design safe).

  • Always forward. A put is forwarded to the cold_store whether or not the key is cached. The cache never answers a create-only put, a 412 or an existence check from its own state: an object that was cached and then deleted at the origin, for example by the orphan sweep, would otherwise be reported present, or refused.
  • Publish after the acknowledgement. A 128 MiB put streams through the node. The local copy is written to a temporary file and renamed into place only after the cold_store acknowledges, or the node reads it back from the origin. A local file must not be readable before the acknowledgement, or the node would serve an object the system never accepted.
  • Which puts are kept. The design says a put is kept locally, and it says the cache holds digest-named keys. The keys the frontend mints for a task’s pack, cas/b/<lease>/<nonce>-0, carry a lease and a MAC and not a digest, so on the second rule they do not qualify. A put of such a key will be forwarded and acknowledged by the cold_store in every case; whether the node then keeps it is open, and the paper does not state both. Until it is settled, the byte-budget row below does not say whether outputs count.
  • How a key is judged digest-named (a name pattern, or a verified hash) is open.
  • An upload abandoned by a dying node leaves its parts on the cold store. Only the listing of multipart uploads that the GC key uses can find them, and no sweeper runs.

What the cache should refuse to cache (our requirements)

Only the first rule, digest-named and create-only keys, is the core design. The other three are our requirements.

  • Any key that is not digest-named. The task-pack keys above do not qualify as they are.
  • The reserved prefixes _journal/ and _tiering/: they hold objects that are rewritten, and the front refuses them with 403, so they cannot arrive through the chain.
  • A miss. A “not found” is never cached, because the key can be created a moment later.
  • A listing. A listing changes as keys are put and deleted. We would pass it through to the cold_store; the core design does not say.

Design requirements the cache inherits from section 2

The proxy’s read path shows what a node cache must not copy, and a chain makes two of them worse because a miss crosses a second proxy. These are requirements, not features. Section 5 lists the full set that the repeat-read workload asks of the hierarchy, including read-aware eviction and no cold existence check for digest-named keys on the storage side; the three below are the ones that fall on the cache. The design requires each.

  • Single-flight fills. Concurrent misses of one key on a node must share one fill. Several build nodes starting empty on the same toolchain at once need a plan so that they do not send that many times the requests to the storage tierstores, which themselves have no single-flight (section 2).
  • Range or chunk fills. A fill must not read and store a 128 MiB pack to serve a 4 KiB blob. Behind it, a storage tierstore that serves a cold object reads the whole object and copies it into hot (section 2); a ranged fill from the build node saves the build node’s bytes and none of the storage side’s.
  • An admission rule, and read-aware eviction. Written-through outputs and big one-off inputs must not displace the inputs that are read again, and a sweep must not promote everything it touches. In measurement 1 the most-read blob is read about 1,600 times per node and the second about 1,000, so evicting one of them is the costly mistake. The rule we chose, plain LRU, keeps what is read; what it admits is open beyond what the design proposes. The design admits by key prefix, so packs and whole large objects are kept and anything else passes through, and a ranged read of an object not held fills the whole object only up to 64 MiB. Whether a written-through output or a large one-off input needs a stricter test is an option, for example admission on the second read, and would be decided by the repeat counts of a day of builds.

Open design questions for the local_cache, placement decided

Each item below is open in the part it names; where another part is decided, the item says so. Decided: the local_cache is on the node SSD in front of the cold_store and writes through. The items below are open in the part each names.

  • What is evicted, and what pins an entry. An entry that a running job has mounted must not be evicted. How the tierstore knows which entries those are is open, and so is the byte budget.
  • How a key becomes digest-named, and who checks. The key the frontend code mints for a task’s pack is under cas/b/ and carries a lease and a MAC, not a digest. What a job wants is a blob, and what is stored is a pack of up to 128 MiB. The cache could hold packs and ranges of packs, or blobs by digest under a layout that can be hard-linked, filled from packs. How a pack becomes hard-linkable files is open, and we do not claim that hard-linked views will work. Who checks the digest, the writer on the write path, the cache on fill, or the consumer, is open (above).
  • Crash-consistent fills and verification. A cached entry is served without going back to the store, so a fill has to survive a crash or a power loss: a temporary file, a sync, a rename, a directory sync. A torn entry after a power loss is not caught by checking at fill time, because the fill had ended; it must be checked on open, or the node must use a file system that checksums data. XFS and ext4 do not detect bit rot of file data.
  • Inodes and fill rate. A toolchain is about 18,000 small files. What that costs in inodes and directory size, and how fast a node disk fills, are open.
  • Whether a node with a warm cache may start with the storage tier down, and what a read or a put does then (above).
  • A full node disk, and sharing it. What the cache does when the disk is full, and how long a run waits for space, are open. The job scheduler counts disk as a resource; how the cache’s byte budget and a job’s scratch space share a node’s disk is open.
